Chapter 67 — CCAR-F Study Strategy & Preparation Roadmap CCAR-F

Foundations is the entry-level architect credential: it validates that you can design and build production-grade Claude solutions, not merely discuss them. It is implementation-heavy despite the "Architect" title.

67.1 Exam Format (items, scenarios, duration) ↑ TOC

Notes. CCAR-F is a proctored, scenario-based exam. Community sources consistently report a fixed-form multiple-choice / multiple-response structure with no hands-on lab. Questions are framed as short design scenarios ("A team needs X; which approach…") rather than trivia recall.

💡 Tip: With ~60 items in 120 minutes you have ~2 minutes per question. Do a first pass answering everything you know in <90 seconds, flag the rest, and reserve the final ~15 minutes for flagged items. Common misunderstanding: candidates assume "Architect" means a hands-on lab exam — it is not; it is scenario multiple-choice, so practice reading design vignettes, not coding under time pressure.
🎯 Strategy: For multi-response items, the stem usually tells you how many to pick ("Select TWO"). Evaluate each option as an independent true/false statement rather than ranking them against each other — this avoids over-selecting.

Mini-scenario

"You have 60 items and hit a dense MCP-design question at #12 that you can't resolve in 30 seconds." → Flag it, pick your best guess now (never leave blank — no negative marking is reported), and move on. Returning with fresh context after easier questions often makes the answer obvious.

67.2 Scoring Methodology (scaled range, pass threshold) ↑ TOC

Notes. Like most Pearson VUE professional exams, CCAR-F is reported to use a scaled score (raw points transformed onto a fixed scale so different form versions are comparable). You receive pass/fail plus a scaled number; individual question weights are not published.

Scoring model ILLUSTRATIVE — verify against official Exam Guide
ElementReported valueInterpretation
Scaled score range100–1000Raw score mapped to a fixed scale
Pass threshold720 / 1000Same value used everywhere in this guide
Negative markingNone reportedAlways answer every item
Result timingOn-screen at end (typical Pearson VUE)Provisional pending review
💡 Tip: A scaled 720/1000 does not mean "72% of questions correct." Because of scaling, the raw-correct percentage needed can differ. Common misunderstanding: candidates target exactly 72% raw and cut it too close — aim for ≥80% on practice sets to build a safety margin against scaling and a hard form.
🎯 Strategy: Since weights are hidden, treat every question as equally valuable. Don't "spend" disproportionate time chasing one hard item — protecting three easy correct answers elsewhere is a better score-per-minute trade.

Worked example

Practice-test raw = 45/60 correct = 75%. Feels like a pass against "72%," but scaling on a hard form could push the passing raw toward ~78%. Verdict: borderline — review weak domains before booking.

Chapter 68 — CCAR-P Study Strategy & Preparation Roadmap CCAR-P

Professional is the advanced architect credential: it validates end-to-end ownership — discovery, design, build, deployment, governance, and stakeholder delivery — across the full solution lifecycle. Even less is publicly documented about CCAR-P than CCAR-F, so treat this chapter's specifics as doubly illustrative.

68.1 Exam Format ↑ TOC

Notes. Reported as a proctored scenario exam like CCAR-F, but with heavier case-study items — longer vignettes that ask you to make trade-off decisions across an entire architecture rather than a single component.

CCAR-P format ILLUSTRATIVE — even less documented than CCAR-F
AttributeReported / assumed valueStatus
Question count~60 items (assumed parity with CCAR-F)Illustrative
Item typesMultiple-choice, multi-response, extended case studyIllustrative
Duration120 minutesIllustrative
PrerequisiteCCAR-F commonly assumed; confirm officiallyIllustrative
DeliveryPearson VUE — OnVUE or test centerVerified
💡 Tip: Case-study items may share one long stem across several questions. Read the whole scenario once before answering any sub-question. Common misunderstanding: answering sub-questions in isolation and missing a constraint stated earlier in the shared stem.
🎯 Strategy: For case studies, jot the 2–3 hard constraints (budget, latency, compliance) on your scratch board first; then every answer must satisfy all of them — this eliminates otherwise-attractive distractors.

Mini-scenario

A 400-word case says "must run in-region for GDPR." Three of four options are technically sound but only one keeps data in-region. The compliance constraint, not the architecture elegance, decides it.

68.5 Bridging Foundations → Professional Knowledge ↑ TOC

Notes. The mental shift is from "can I build this component?" (F) to "can I own this system in production and justify it to a business?" (P).

CCAR-F vs. CCAR-P — comparison chart

What changes as you move up a level
DimensionCCAR-F (Foundations)CCAR-P (Professional)
ScopeComponents & single agentsEnd-to-end solutions & lifecycle
Question styleShort scenariosExtended case studies
GovernanceBasics (~10%)Core driver (~20%)
Deliverable focusWorking buildBuild + risk + stakeholder sign-off
Key question"Does it work?""Should we ship it, and who owns the risk?"

💡 Tip: Re-take a CCAR-F practice question and ask "what would the Professional version of this add?" — usually cost, compliance, monitoring, or a stakeholder. Common misunderstanding: assuming P is just "harder F questions" rather than a wider scope.
🎯 Strategy: When a P-level option looks identical to an F-level "correct" answer, prefer the option that also addresses operations or risk — that added dimension is usually what P is testing.

Mini-scenario

F answer: "Use a tool loop with retry." P answer to the same setup: "Use a tool loop with retry, add a dead-letter queue, alerting, and a fallback to human review" — same core, wider ownership.

Chapter 69 — CCAR-F Preparation Exercises CCAR-F

Four build exercises mapped to the heaviest Foundations domains. Each is scoped to a single session and lists the exam-relevant failure modes it exposes.

69.1 Multi-Tool Agent with Escalation Logic ↑ TOC

Notes. Build an agent with ≥2 tools (e.g. weather + calendar) plus an escalation path to a human when confidence is low, a tool fails repeatedly, or the request is out of scope. Core concepts: the tool-use loop, stop conditions, and graceful degradation.

Escalation decision flow

User request
→ Need a tool? → Call tool → Success & confident? → Answer user
Fail ×N or low confidence → Escalate to human
💡 Tip: Always define a max-iteration cap and an explicit "no tool needed → final answer" branch. Common misunderstanding: assuming the model will stop on its own — without a stop condition, agents loop or over-call tools.
🎯 Strategy: On agent-debugging questions, first locate the termination / escalation logic in the described loop before judging the tool schemas — missing stop conditions are the single most common seeded fault.

Worked example

Escalation rule: if tool_failures >= 2 or confidence < 0.6: route_to_human(). A test asks why an agent "spams" a flaky API — answer: no failure cap before escalation.

69.2 Configuring Claude Code for a Team Project ↑ TOC

Notes. Set up a shared repo so every teammate gets consistent behavior: a committed project config, a CLAUDE.md conventions file, agreed tool/permission scopes, and secrets kept out of the config.

Team Claude Code setup checklist
ItemPurposePitfall if skipped
Committed project configEveryone shares tools & settings"Works on my machine" drift
CLAUDE.md conventionsCoding style, do/don't rulesInconsistent output across devs
Permission scopingLeast-privilege tool accessUnsafe/destructive actions
Secrets via env, not configKeeps keys out of gitLeaked credentials
💡 Tip: Commit shared config but never commit secrets — reference environment variables. Common misunderstanding: putting API keys in the committed config "just to get it working."
🎯 Strategy: For config questions, the correct answer almost always favors shared + version-controlled + least-privilege. Options that centralize secrets in code or grant blanket permissions are distractors.

Mini-scenario

A teammate gets different results from the same prompt. Cause: their local config overrides the repo config. Fix: commit a canonical config and document precedence in CLAUDE.md.

69.3 Structured Data Extraction Pipeline ↑ TOC

Notes. Turn unstructured text (invoices, emails) into validated JSON. Core concepts: enforce a schema, validate output, and handle fields the model can't find (null vs. hallucinate).

Raw document→ Prompt + JSON schema→ Claude extracts→ Validate against schema→ Store
Validation fails→ Retry / flag for review
💡 Tip: Instruct the model to return null for missing fields rather than guessing. Common misunderstanding: assuming valid JSON = correct data — always add a schema-validation step, since well-formed JSON can still contain hallucinated values.
🎯 Strategy: When options differ only by whether they validate output, pick the one that validates. Extraction items reward defensive pipelines, not just a clever prompt.

Worked example

Invoice missing a PO number. Bad pipeline invents "PO-0000." Good pipeline returns "po_number": null and routes to review — the exam-correct behavior.

69.4 Multi-Agent Research Pipeline Design & Debugging ↑ TOC

Notes. Coordinate specialized agents (planner → researchers → synthesizer). Core concepts: task decomposition, parallel vs. sequential steps, and where a pipeline breaks (context loss between hops, duplicated work, no aggregation step).

Planner→
Researcher A Researcher B
→ Synthesizer→ Report
💡 Tip: Ensure the synthesizer receives each sub-agent's output, not just its task. Common misunderstanding: assuming shared "memory" — sub-agents are usually isolated, so context must be explicitly passed forward.
🎯 Strategy: In pipeline-debugging questions, identify the failure point in the diagram first, then evaluate answer choices against that node — don't grade the whole pipeline at once.

Mini-scenario

Final report omits Researcher B's findings. Failure point = the synthesizer only reads A's output. Fix = pass both outputs into the synthesis step (an aggregation/fan-in bug, not a prompt bug).

Chapter 70 — CCAR-P Preparation Exercises CCAR-P

Four Professional-level exercises emphasizing lifecycle ownership: architecture, evaluation, governance, and delivery.

70.1 Full Solution Architecture from a Business Brief ↑ TOC

Notes. Translate a business brief ("reduce support ticket handling time by 40%") into an architecture: components, data flow, model choices, integrations, and non-functional requirements (latency, cost, compliance).

Business goal→ Requirements (NFRs)→ Component design→ Integration & data flow→ Success metrics
💡 Tip: Tie every component back to the business metric. Common misunderstanding: designing an elegant system that never states how it moves the target KPI — P-level answers must connect architecture to outcome.
🎯 Strategy: Extract explicit constraints (budget, latency, compliance) from the brief first; any option violating a stated constraint is wrong regardless of technical merit.

Worked example

Brief: cut handling time 40%, keep data in-region, <$0.05/ticket. A cheaper cross-region design is disqualified by the in-region constraint even though it hits cost and latency.

70.2 End-to-End RAG Pipeline Build & Evaluation ↑ TOC

Notes. Build ingest → chunk → embed → retrieve → generate, then evaluate with retrieval metrics (recall@k) and answer metrics (faithfulness/groundedness). The evaluation loop is the P-level differentiator.

Ingest→ Chunk→ Embed→ Retrieve→ Generate→ Evaluate
What to measure — and what a bad score usually means
MetricMeasuresLow score points to…
Recall@kDid retrieval fetch the right chunks?Chunking / embedding / index
FaithfulnessIs the answer grounded in retrieved text?Generation prompt / hallucination
Answer relevanceDoes it address the question?Query understanding
💡 Tip: Diagnose retrieval and generation separately. If recall@k is high but faithfulness is low, the retriever is fine — fix the prompt. Common misunderstanding: "blaming the model" for what is actually a retrieval/chunking failure.
🎯 Strategy: For RAG-failure scenarios, ask "were the right chunks retrieved?" first. High recall + wrong answer = generation problem; low recall = retrieval problem. This split maps directly to the answer options.

Worked example

Answers cite the wrong policy version. Recall@k shows the correct chunk was never retrieved → root cause is chunk size splitting the version header from its clause; fix chunking, not the prompt.

70.3 Governance and Risk Assessment ↑ TOC

Notes. Produce a risk register: identify risks (PII exposure, hallucination, prompt injection, cost overrun), rate likelihood × impact, and assign mitigations and owners.

Sample risk register (excerpt)
RiskLikelihood × ImpactMitigationOwner
PII leakage in outputsHigh × HighRedaction at ingest + output filterData lead
Prompt injection via docsMed × HighInput sanitization, tool allow-listSecurity
Hallucinated answersMed × MedRAG grounding + faithfulness evalsML lead
Cost overrunLow × MedToken budgets, caching, alertsPlatform
💡 Tip: Every risk needs a named owner and a mitigation — a risk with neither is just a worry. Common misunderstanding: listing risks without ownership or controls, which P-level questions treat as an incomplete assessment.
🎯 Strategy: Rank by likelihood × impact, then answer "which risk do we mitigate first?" with the highest product — not the scariest-sounding one. Distractors exploit fear over math.

Mini-scenario

Team wants to fix a low×low edge case first because it's novel. Correct P-level move: mitigate High×High PII leakage first — impact-weighted prioritization.

70.4 Presenting Architecture Decisions to Stakeholders ↑ TOC

Notes. Communicate a decision to a mixed audience (executives + engineers): state the decision, the options considered, the trade-offs, and the recommendation with its business justification. A one-page decision brief / ADR is the standard artifact.

Decision brief structure (tailor depth to audience)
SectionExecutive cares aboutEngineer cares about
DecisionWhat & why, in one linePrecise scope
Options & trade-offsCost, risk, timelineTechnical constraints
RecommendationBusiness impactImplementation path
Risks & mitigationsWhat could go wrongOwner & controls
💡 Tip: Lead with the recommendation and its business impact, then support with detail — don't make executives read the reasoning to find the answer. Common misunderstanding: presenting deep technical detail first and burying the decision.
🎯 Strategy: For "how do you present to X?" items, match message to audience: executives → outcomes, cost, risk; engineers → constraints, implementation. The best answer tailors depth, not just content.

Worked example

To a CFO: "Option B costs 20% more up front but cuts per-ticket cost 40% — payback in 4 months." To engineers on the same choice: latency budget, model selection, and the migration plan. Same decision, two framings.
