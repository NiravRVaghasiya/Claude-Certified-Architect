57.1 Bias Detection and Mitigation in LLM Outputs

Bias in an LLM is any systematic skew in outputs that unfairly advantages or disadvantages a group, idea, or attribute. It enters through training data, model architecture, or the human feedback loop — and must be measured, not assumed.

Types of Bias

Common bias types on the exam — know the source, not just the name.
TypeWhere it comes fromLLM example
Representation / SamplingTraining corpus over- or under-represents a groupFewer texts in a language → weaker, error-prone responses for its speakers
HistoricalData faithfully reflects past inequitiesAssociating "nurse" with women, "engineer" with men
MeasurementThe label or proxy is a poor stand-in for the real targetUsing "arrest rate" as a proxy for "crime," encoding policing bias
AggregationOne model applied to subgroups that behave differentlyA single tone model that misreads dialect-specific phrasing
ConfirmationModel (or prompt) reinforces a pre-existing assumptionLeading prompts that elicit a stereotype the model then "agrees" with
AutomationHumans over-trust the model's outputReviewer rubber-stamps an AI hiring recommendation without checking

Detection Techniques

  • Statistical parity checks — compare output rates (e.g., positive/negative sentiment, approval) across demographic slices.
  • Counterfactual / output diffing — swap a sensitive attribute in an otherwise identical prompt ("He is a great leader" vs. "She is a great leader") and diff the outputs.
  • Prompt-based probing & red-teaming — adversarial prompts designed to elicit biased or unsafe completions.
  • Embedding association tests — e.g., WEAT (Word Embedding Association Test) measures stereotype strength in the vector space.
  • Benchmark datasets — established bias probes such as BOLD, StereoSet, and BBQ (Bias Benchmark for QA).

Mitigation Strategies (across the lifecycle)

1Pre-processing
Data curation & balancing
→
2In-training
Fine-tuning, RLHF, Constitutional AI
→
3Post-processing
Output filtering & guardrails
  • Data curation — de-duplicate, rebalance, and filter toxic/biased training data.
  • Fine-tuning & RLHF — align behavior with human preference signals; Anthropic's Constitutional AI uses a written set of principles to self-critique and revise outputs.
  • System prompts & guardrails — steer behavior at inference time and filter/refuse harmful outputs.
  • Output filtering / classifiers — a secondary model screens generations before they reach the user.
💡 Tips & Tricks Remember the mitigation lifecycle as "PIP": Pre-process the data → In-training alignment (RLHF/Constitutional AI) → Post-process the output. Any single answer choice that "fixes bias with one step" is almost always wrong — bias is managed at multiple stages.
🎯 Exam Strategy Questions often describe a symptom and ask for the best intervention. Map the symptom to the lifecycle stage: skewed training data → data curation; undesired learned behavior → fine-tuning/RLHF; a leaked harmful response → output filtering/guardrails. Match the fix to where the problem originates.
⚠️ Common Pitfalls Don't confuse bias (a systematic, measurable skew) with variance or random error. Also, "remove the sensitive attribute" (fairness through unawareness) is a trap answer — proxies (ZIP code, name, language) let bias persist even after the attribute is dropped.

57.2 Fairness: Ensuring Equitable Outcomes Across User Groups

Fairness is not one metric — it is a family of mathematically distinct, often conflicting definitions. The exam tests whether you can pick the right definition for a scenario and reason about the trade-offs.

Individual vs. Group Fairness

  • Individual fairness — similar individuals should receive similar outcomes (treat like cases alike).
  • Group fairness — outcomes should be statistically comparable across protected groups.

Group Fairness Metrics

The three most-tested group-fairness definitions.
MetricWhat it equalizesUse when…
Demographic (statistical) parityPositive-outcome rate is equal across groupsYou want equal selection regardless of ground truth (e.g., outreach)
Equal opportunityTrue-positive rate (recall) is equal across groupsMissing a qualified person is the main harm (e.g., loan/hiring for the deserving)
Equalized oddsBoth TPR and FPR are equal across groupsBoth false negatives and false positives carry real cost
Predictive parityPrecision (PPV) is equal across groupsYou care that a "positive" prediction means the same thing for everyone

The Core Trade-off (Impossibility)

A foundational result (Kleinberg et al.; Chouldechova) shows that when base rates differ across groups, you cannot simultaneously satisfy demographic parity, equalized odds, and predictive parity — except in trivial cases. Choosing a fairness metric is therefore a value judgment, not a purely technical one.

Illustrative: fairness–accuracy tension

Accuracy-only model
92%
Fairness-constrained
88%
Disparity gap (lower = fairer)
↓

Illustrative pattern only (not real benchmark data): enforcing a fairness constraint often trades a small amount of aggregate accuracy for a large reduction in group disparity.

💡 Tips & Tricks Memorize the metrics with "D-E-E": Demographic parity (equal rate), Equal opportunity (equal TPR), Equalized odds (equal TPR + FPR). Mnemonic bridge: Opportunity = one rate (TPR); Odds = two rates (TPR & FPR).
🎯 Exam Strategy Elimination strategy for metric questions: read what harm the scenario emphasizes. If it stresses qualified people being missed → equal opportunity. If it stresses equal representation regardless of qualification → demographic parity. If it says "must balance both error types" → equalized odds. Eliminate any option that only equalizes one rate when the scenario names two.
⚠️ Common Pitfalls The classic trap: assuming you can "just satisfy all fairness metrics at once." The impossibility theorem forbids it under differing base rates. Also don't equate demographic parity with "fair" universally — enforcing equal rates can force unqualified selections and is inappropriate when ground-truth qualification legitimately differs.

57.3 Transparency: Explaining AI Decisions to Stakeholders

Transparency means stakeholders can understand what a system does, why it produced an output, and what its limits are — communicated at the right level for each audience.

Explainability vs. Interpretability

InterpretabilityExplainability
DefinitionThe degree to which a human can inherently understand the model's mechanicsPost-hoc techniques that explain a specific decision after the fact
Typical modelSimple/"glass-box" (linear, decision tree)Complex "black-box" (deep nets, LLMs)
ExampleReading a decision tree's rules directlySHAP/LIME feature attributions; chain-of-thought rationales

Rule of thumb: interpretability is a property of the model; explainability is a tool applied to the model.

Techniques & Artifacts

  • Model Cards (Mitchell et al., 2019) — document intended use, performance across groups, and limitations.
  • Datasheets for Datasets (Gebru et al.) — document a dataset's provenance, composition, and collection process.
  • System Cards — model-provider documents (used by Anthropic/OpenAI) describing capabilities, safety evaluations, and risks.
  • Decision / audit logs — record inputs, outputs, model version, and rationale for traceability.
  • Feature attribution — SHAP (Shapley-value based) and LIME (local surrogate) for per-prediction explanations.
  • Natural-language explanations & chain-of-thought — human-readable reasoning (with the caveat that stated reasoning may not equal true internal process).

Stakeholder-Specific Communication

AudienceWhat they needBest artifact
ML engineers / data scientistsMechanics, metrics, failure modesSHAP plots, model card metrics, eval reports
Executives / productRisk, business impact, limitsModel card summary, risk register
End users / affected peoplePlain-language reason & recourseNatural-language explanation, appeal path
Regulators / auditorsTraceability & compliance evidenceDecision logs, datasheets, governance records
💡 Tips & Tricks Split the words to remember them: Interpretability = the model is inherently readable (built-in). Explainability = you explain a black box afterward. "Inherent = Interpretable, After-the-fact = Explainable."
🎯 Exam Strategy When a question names an audience, match the explanation depth to it. A non-technical stakeholder never needs SHAP values — pick plain-language / model-card summaries. Answers that give regulators "a chain-of-thought log" or end users "raw attribution scores" are distractors that mismatch audience and artifact.
⚠️ Common Pitfalls Treating a model's chain-of-thought as a faithful account of its internal computation — it is a plausible narrative, not verified mechanism. And don't swap the terms: interpretability ≠ explainability (a frequent definition-matching trap).

57.4 Responsible AI Frameworks and Organizational Policies

Frameworks give structure to "do AI responsibly." Know the NIST AI RMF functions, the OECD principles, and the EU AI Act risk tiers — these three appear most on the exam.

Major Frameworks

NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0)

A voluntary US framework built on four core functions:

1GOVERN
Culture, roles, accountability
→
2MAP
Context & risk identification
→
3MEASURE
Analyze, assess, track
→
4MANAGE
Prioritize & respond

Govern is cross-cutting — it wraps the other three, not just a first step.

OECD AI Principles

Five values-based principles adopted by many governments (basis for the G20 principles):

Inclusive growth & well-being Human-centred values & fairness Transparency & explainability Robustness, security & safety Accountability

EU AI Act — Risk Tiers

Risk-based regulation: obligations scale with risk.
TierTreatmentExample
UnacceptableBannedSocial scoring, most real-time public biometric ID
HighStrict obligations (conformity assessment, oversight, documentation)Hiring, credit scoring, medical devices
LimitedTransparency duties (disclose AI use)Chatbots, deepfakes must be labeled
MinimalLargely unregulatedSpam filters, AI in video games

Also worth knowing: ISO/IEC 42001 (AI management-system standard) and the OECD/G7 and UNESCO recommendations.

Components of an Internal Responsible-AI Policy

  • Scope & principles (values the org commits to)
  • Risk classification & impact-assessment process
  • Data governance (provenance, privacy, consent)
  • Model documentation requirements (model cards, datasheets)
  • Human oversight & escalation paths
  • Monitoring, incident response, and audit cadence

Governance Roles

RoleResponsibility
AI Ethics Board / CommitteeSets policy, reviews high-risk use cases, resolves value trade-offs
Model Risk OwnerAccountable for a specific model's risk throughout its lifecycle
Responsible-AI / ML Ops leadImplements controls, monitoring, and documentation
Data Protection OfficerPrivacy, consent, and regulatory compliance
💡 Tips & Tricks NIST AI RMF functions = "GMMM" → Govern, Map, Measure, Manage. Say it as "Govern wraps Map → Measure → Manage." For the EU AI Act, remember the ladder U-H-L-M (Unacceptable → High → Limited → Minimal) = "banned → strict → disclose → free."
🎯 Exam Strategy Framework questions are usually matching ("which function/tier applies?"). Anchor on keywords: "identify context/risks" = Map; "quantify/track metrics" = Measure; "chatbot must disclose it's AI" = EU Limited risk; "used for hiring decisions" = EU High risk. NIST is voluntary; the EU AI Act is binding law — don't mix those attributes.
⚠️ Common Pitfalls Confusing NIST's Map / Measure / Manage (three similar-sounding M's) — Map = identify, Measure = quantify, Manage = act. Also, don't call the OECD principles or NIST RMF "regulations" — they're voluntary/guidance; only instruments like the EU AI Act are enforceable law.

57.5 Hands-On: Conducting a Bias Audit

A bias audit is a structured, repeatable evaluation of a deployed model for disparate outcomes across groups. Know the workflow, the checklist, and the headline metric — the disparate impact ratio.

Step-by-Step Audit Methodology

1Define Scope
→
2Collect Data
→
3Run Tests
→
4Analyze
→
5Report
→
6Remediate
  1. Define scope — the use case, protected attributes, chosen fairness metric(s), and pass/fail thresholds before looking at results.
  2. Collect data — a representative, labeled evaluation set with group membership (or reliable proxies), sized for statistical power per subgroup.
  3. Run tests — compute per-group performance and fairness metrics; include counterfactual and adversarial probes.
  4. Analyze — identify disparities, test for statistical significance, and trace likely root cause (data vs. model vs. threshold).
  5. Report — document findings, methodology, and limitations (e.g., in a model card / audit report) for stakeholders.
  6. Remediate & monitor — apply mitigation (re-balance, re-train, re-threshold, add guardrails), then set up ongoing monitoring for drift.

The Headline Metric: Disparate Impact Ratio

The disparate impact (DI) ratio = (selection rate of the disadvantaged group) ÷ (selection rate of the advantaged group). Under the US EEOC four-fifths (80%) rule, a ratio below 0.80 is a common red flag for adverse impact.

Group A selection rate
50%
Group B selection rate
35%
DI ratio (35/50 = 0.70)
0.70 ✗

Illustrative: 0.70 < 0.80 threshold → fails the four-fifths rule, signaling potential adverse impact worth investigating.

Sample Audit Checklist

  • Scope, protected attributes, and fairness metric(s) defined and documented
  • Pass/fail thresholds agreed before analysis (pre-registration)
  • Evaluation dataset is representative, labeled, and sufficiently large per subgroup
  • Per-group performance + fairness metrics computed (incl. DI ratio)
  • Counterfactual / adversarial probes executed
  • Disparities tested for statistical significance
  • Root cause traced (data / model / threshold)
  • Findings, methods, and limitations reported to stakeholders
  • Remediation plan defined and ongoing monitoring scheduled

Tools & Metrics Commonly Used

Fairlearn (Microsoft) AI Fairness 360 / AIF360 (IBM) What-If Tool (Google) Aequitas

Key metrics: disparate impact ratio, demographic parity difference, equal-opportunity difference, equalized-odds difference, per-group precision/recall.

💡 Tips & Tricks Memorize the audit flow as "S-C-R-A-R-R": Scope → Collect → Run → Analyze → Report → Remediate. And nail the number: four-fifths = 0.80 is the disparate-impact threshold.
🎯 Exam Strategy Expect a numeric item: given two selection rates, compute the ratio (disadvantaged ÷ advantaged) and compare to 0.80. Also watch for "what's the first step?" — the answer is almost always define scope / metric (never "start remediating"). Defining thresholds up front is the anti-cherry-picking control.
⚠️ Common Pitfalls Inverting the DI ratio (always put the disadvantaged group's rate in the numerator). Auditing on a tiny or unrepresentative sample and over-reading noise. And treating a one-time audit as sufficient — bias re-emerges with data drift, so monitoring must be continuous.
