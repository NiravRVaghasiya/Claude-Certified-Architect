59.1

Presenting Technical Architecture to Non-Technical Audiences

The value of an architecture is capped by how well the people who fund and depend on it understand it. Your job is not to simplify the system — it's to translate it into the listener's world (outcomes, risk, cost, timelines) without distorting the truth.

Overview / Definition

Presenting technical architecture to a non-technical audience means expressing what the system does, why it's built this way, and what it means for the business — using the audience's vocabulary (revenue, risk, effort, customer impact) instead of implementation vocabulary (embeddings, vector stores, token budgets). The architecture doesn't change; the abstraction level does.

Why it matters in real AI architecture roles

  • Funding & prioritization: Executives approve budgets based on understood value, not technical elegance.
  • Trust & adoption: Business owners who understand the system's boundaries defend it and use it correctly.
  • Expectation control: Clear framing up front prevents "why can't it just be 100% accurate?" conversations later (see 59.3).
  • Influence: The architect who can explain a RAG pipeline in one sentence to a VP has more organizational leverage than one who can only explain it to peers.

Key frameworks

  • BLUF — Bottom Line Up Front: Lead with the recommendation/outcome, then support it. Non-technical audiences want the conclusion first, detail on demand.
  • Minto Pyramid Principle: Answer → key supporting arguments → evidence. Structure top-down, not bottom-up.
  • The Abstraction Ladder: Consciously choose the rung — business outcome → capability → component → implementation. Match the rung to the audience and only descend when asked.
  • Feature → Benefit → Outcome ("So what?" ladder): Never stop at the feature. "We use prompt caching (feature) → so repeated context is 90% cheaper to process (benefit) → so we can afford to run this at full scale within budget (outcome)."
  • Analogy-first explanation: Anchor an unfamiliar concept to a familiar one before adding nuance.

Technical vs. non-technical communication styles

❌ Engineer-to-engineer framing

  • "We chunk docs, embed with a bi-encoder, store in a vector DB, and do top-k retrieval with a cross-encoder reranker."
  • Leads with mechanism.
  • Assumes shared vocabulary.
  • Measures success in latency/recall.

✅ Non-technical framing

  • "It's like giving the assistant an open-book exam using our documents, so answers stay grounded in our policies."
  • Leads with outcome and analogy.
  • Uses the audience's vocabulary.
  • Measures success in cost, trust, customer impact.
Worked Example

Scenario: You must get a VP of Customer Support to fund a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) assistant for agents.

Weak (feature-first): "We'll build a RAG pipeline with semantic search over your knowledge base and inject retrieved chunks into Claude's context window."

Strong (BLUF + analogy + outcome): "Recommendation: fund a help-desk assistant that drafts answers from our own support docs. Think of it as an open-book assistant — it only answers using our approved articles, and it shows the source so agents can verify in one click. Expected impact: faster first responses and fewer escalations, at a cost we can cap per conversation. It won't replace agents; it makes each agent faster."

Notice: conclusion first, familiar analogy, explicit boundary ("won't replace agents"), and business metrics — no mention of embeddings unless the VP asks.

💡 Tips & Tricks
  • Mnemonic — A.B.C.: Audience (who + what they care about) → Benefit (outcome, not feature) → Confidence (what's proven vs. assumed, and the boundaries).
  • Analogy library: RAG = open-book exam; fine-tuning = on-the-job training; guardrails = a bouncer at the door; prompt = the job brief; context window = short-term memory / desk space.
  • One diagram, one arrow of value: Show inputs → system → business outcome, not the full component graph.
  • Pre-empt the "why not 100%?" question before it's asked (bridges into 59.3).
⚠️ Common Pitfalls
  • Jargon leakage: "vector", "token", "embedding", "inference" mean nothing to most stakeholders — and using them signals you can't translate.
  • Over-simplifying into a lie: "It just knows the answer" is false and sets you up to fail in 59.3. Simplify the level, not the truth.
  • Bottom-up storytelling: Building up to the conclusion loses a busy executive. Lead with it.
  • One-size framing: The same deck for the CFO, the legal team, and the engineering lead will underserve all three.
🎯 Exam Strategy — 59.1

Likely question types: Scenario ("You're presenting X to a non-technical exec — which framing is best?"), MCQ on which statement avoids jargon, short-answer on choosing an analogy.

Answer structure: Identify the audience & their priority → choose the abstraction rung → lead with outcome (BLUF) → add one analogy → state the boundary.

Graders look for: "audience-appropriate", "outcome / business value first", "analogy", "avoid jargon", "set boundaries / manage expectations", "BLUF / pyramid".

59.2

Communicating Tradeoffs: Cost vs Quality vs Latency vs Safety

Every LLM architecture decision moves a slider between four competing dimensions. You rarely maximize all four — the architect's job is to make the tradeoff explicit, quantified, and tied to the use case, so stakeholders choose consciously instead of assuming "best on everything".

Overview / Definition

The four core dimensions of an LLM system:

  • Cost — spend per request/token; total run-rate at scale.
  • Quality — accuracy, relevance, completeness, reasoning depth of outputs.
  • Latency — time to first token and time to full response; user-perceived speed.
  • Safety — resistance to harmful, non-compliant, or hallucinated output; auditability and control.

These trade against one another: a larger, more capable model tends to raise quality and cost and latency; heavy guardrails and human review raise safety but add latency and cost. There is no free lunch — improving one dimension usually spends another.

Why it matters

Stakeholders instinctively want maximum quality, minimum cost, instant responses, and perfect safety. Presenting those as simultaneously achievable is dishonest and erodes trust. Communicating tradeoffs well turns an emotional "make it better and cheaper" demand into a rational "for this use case, we'll optimize for X and accept Y" decision.

The architect's levers (how to move each slider)

Worked Example — two use cases, opposite tradeoffs

Case A — Support ticket triage (10,000/day): Volume is high, each classification is low-stakes, and a human handles the actual reply. Decision: optimize for Cost + Latency — use a smaller/faster tier with caching; accept slightly lower per-item quality because misroutes are caught downstream.

Case B — Clinical documentation summary: Errors carry patient-safety and regulatory risk. Decision: optimize for Safety + Quality — use a more capable tier, ground with retrieval, require clinician sign-off, and log every output. Accept higher cost and latency because the downside of an error dominates.

How you'd say it to stakeholders: "Same technology, opposite dials. For triage we buy scale and speed; for clinical summaries we buy accuracy and control. Trying to run clinical summaries on the triage configuration would be cheap and fast — and unsafe."

💡 Tips & Tricks
  • Mnemonic — "CLASS" the request: Cost, Latency, Accuracy (Quality), Safety, Scale. Rank them 1–4 before proposing an architecture.
  • The "pick two-ish" rule: You can push any two dimensions hard; the others must give. Say this out loud so stakeholders own the choice.
  • Quantify, don't adjective: Replace "faster" and "cheaper" with "~1.2s first token" and "$X per 1,000 requests". Tie targets to SLOs.
  • Perceived vs. real latency: Streaming makes a slow response feel fast — a UX lever, not a compute lever.
⚠️ Common Pitfalls
  • Promising Pareto-perfection: "cheaper, faster, and better" without a caveat is a red flag — it's almost never all three at once.
  • Hiding the sacrifice: Presenting only the upside of a choice. Always name what you're trading away.
  • Optimizing globally instead of per use case: One config for all workloads over-spends on easy tasks and under-protects hard ones — prefer routing/tiering.
  • Ignoring scale: A decision that's fine at 100 requests/day can be ruinous at 1M/day. State the operating volume.
🎯 Exam Strategy — 59.2

Likely question types: Scenario ("Given use case X, which dimension should be prioritized and what's the tradeoff?"), matching levers to effects, MCQ on "which config fits a regulated domain".

Answer structure: State the tradeoff → justify with the use-case constraints (volume, stakes, latency need) → recommend the lever(s) → name what you sacrifice.

Graders look for: "no free lunch", "prioritize per use case", named levers (model tier, caching, batching, RAG, guardrails, human-in-the-loop, streaming), "quantify / SLO", and an explicit sacrifice.

59.3

Setting Realistic Expectations About LLM Capabilities and Limitations

Over-promising is the fastest way to lose stakeholder trust and ship an unsafe system. The architect's ethical and practical duty is to frame LLM output as high-confidence assistance that requires validation — never as an infallible oracle.

Overview / Definition

Expectation-setting means giving stakeholders an accurate mental model of what a large language model (like Claude) reliably does, what it does with caveats, and what it cannot guarantee — before commitments are made. It is the honesty layer that makes 59.1 and 59.2 credible.

What LLMs do well (capabilities)

  • Natural-language understanding and generation; summarization, rewriting, tone/style adaptation.
  • Information extraction and classification from unstructured text.
  • Drafting and transforming content (emails, code, structured data) from instructions.
  • Following complex instructions and adopting a defined role/format.
  • Tool use / function calling — invoking external systems to fetch fresh data or take actions, which mitigates several limitations below.
  • Handling large inputs within a fixed context window (Claude models offer a large context window — commonly 200K tokens — enabling long documents).

What to be honest about (limitations)

  • Hallucination: Models can produce fluent, confident, and false statements. Fluency ≠ correctness.
  • Non-determinism: The same prompt can yield different outputs (especially at higher temperature). Don't promise identical results every run.
  • Knowledge cutoff: A model's built-in knowledge stops at its training cutoff and has no awareness of events after it — unless given fresh data via tools/retrieval.
  • No inherent grounding: Without retrieval or tools, a model has no live access to your systems, databases, or the internet.
  • Context limits: Inputs beyond the context window must be truncated or chunked; important detail can be lost.
  • Prompt sensitivity: Small wording changes can shift output quality — behavior isn't perfectly stable.
  • No guaranteed accuracy: There is no configuration that yields provable 100% correctness. High-stakes outputs need validation.
  • Potential bias & reasoning gaps: Outputs can reflect biases in data and can fail on some multi-step or numerical reasoning without support (tools, verification).

Framing framework — the "capability contract"

Translate each capability into a stakeholder-safe statement plus its mitigation:

Reframe raw capability into an honest promise + mitigation.
Don't sayDo sayMitigation to pair with it
"It knows the answer.""It drafts a high-confidence answer that a person should validate."Human review for high-stakes; confidence surfacing
"It's always accurate.""It's accurate on most cases; we measure the error rate and set a threshold."Evaluation suite + acceptance criteria
"It has up-to-date info.""Its built-in knowledge has a cutoff; live data comes from our tools/retrieval."RAG / tool use / function calling
"It can't make things up.""It can occasionally state false things confidently, so we ground it and cite sources."Retrieval with citations; guardrails
"Same input, same output.""Outputs can vary run-to-run; we constrain format and lower variability where it matters."Lower temperature, structured output, tests
Worked Example — compliance document review

Context (author's domain): A stakeholder asks, "Can the LLM just automatically approve or reject product-compliance submissions?"

Unsafe framing: "Yes — it'll read the docs and decide, fully automated."

Realistic framing: "It can pre-screen submissions and flag likely issues with citations to the exact policy clause — think of it as a fast, tireless first reviewer. It should not make the final approve/reject call in a regulated flow. We'll frame it as high-confidence assistance requiring a human decision, measure its flag accuracy against a labeled set, and log every recommendation for audit. That gives us most of the speed with none of the compliance risk."

This sets a truthful ceiling (assistive, not autonomous), names mitigations (citations, human sign-off, evals, audit logs), and still lands the business value.

💡 Tips & Tricks
  • 💡 Golden rule: Never promise 100% accuracy — always frame LLM output as "high-confidence assistance requiring validation", especially for compliance-sensitive use cases.
  • Mnemonic — "HALT" before promising: Hallucination possible? Accuracy measured (not assumed)? Limits of knowledge cutoff stated? Tools/human-in-the-loop for high stakes?
  • Analogy: An LLM is a brilliant, fast intern — great first drafts, occasionally confidently wrong, always needs a senior review for anything that matters.
  • Replace vibes with evals: "It's good" → "It hits our acceptance threshold on the eval set; here's the residual error rate."
⚠️ Common Pitfalls
  • Confusing fluency with truth: A confident, well-written answer can still be wrong.
  • Promising autonomy in regulated flows: "Fully automated decisioning" in health/legal/finance is a trust and compliance landmine.
  • Ignoring the knowledge cutoff: Assuming the model "knows" recent events without tools/retrieval.
  • No measurement plan: Setting expectations without an eval set means "accurate" is just an opinion.
🎯 Exam Strategy — 59.3

Likely question types: "Which statement sets realistic expectations?" (MCQ), scenario on responding to "can it be 100% accurate?", identifying a limitation + its mitigation.

Answer structure: Acknowledge the capability → state the limitation honestly → pair each limitation with a concrete mitigation → frame as assistive with validation.

Graders look for: "no 100% guarantee", "hallucination", "knowledge cutoff", "non-deterministic", "human-in-the-loop", "evaluation / acceptance threshold", "grounding / RAG / citations", "high-confidence assistance requiring validation".

59.4

Architecture Decision Records (ADRs) for AI Systems

An ADR is a short, immutable document that captures one significant decision, the context that forced it, and its consequences. For AI systems, ADRs must also record model choice, eval criteria, cost/latency budgets, and safety controls — the parts most likely to change and be questioned later.

Overview / Definition

Architecture Decision Records (a practice popularized by Michael Nygard) are lightweight markdown-style documents, one per decision, stored in the repo alongside the code. They answer "why is it built this way?" for future engineers, auditors, and your future self. They are append-only: you don't rewrite history — you supersede an old ADR with a new one.

Why it matters for AI systems specifically

  • Fast-moving decisions: Model tiers, prompts, and providers change often; ADRs preserve the reasoning behind each choice.
  • Auditability & compliance: Regulated AI use needs a paper trail of why a model, guardrail, or human-review step was chosen.
  • Tradeoff memory: ADRs record the Cost/Quality/Latency/Safety tradeoff (59.2) that was accepted — so nobody re-litigates it blindly.
  • Onboarding: New team members understand the "why", not just the "what".

Standard ADR structure (+ AI extensions)

Classic ADR fields plus fields that matter for AI systems.
FieldWhat it capturesAI-specific note
Title & numberSequential ID + one-line decision (e.g. ADR-014: Model tier for support bot)One decision per ADR
StatusProposed → Accepted → Deprecated / SupersededLink to superseding ADR when replaced
ContextForces at play: requirements, constraints, volume, stakesInclude latency/cost budget, data sensitivity, regulatory constraints
DecisionThe choice made, stated actively ("We will…")Model tier, prompt/version strategy, guardrails, fallback behavior
ConsequencesResulting context — positive and negativeAccepted tradeoff (59.2), monitoring/eval plan, residual risk

ADR lifecycle

Sample ADR (rendered)

ADR-014: Model tier selection for the Customer Support Bot

Status: Accepted Context

~8,000 support conversations/day. Most are routine FAQ-style questions; a minority are complex account issues. Budget target < $0.02 per conversation; users expect a reply to begin in under ~2 seconds. Answers must stay grounded in our published help center; no medical/legal/financial advice.

Decision

We will use a two-tier routing approach: a fast, lower-cost model tier (Haiku-class) handles the majority of FAQ queries with RAG over the help center; queries the router flags as complex escalate to a more capable tier (Sonnet-class). All answers include source citations, and out-of-scope topics are refused via guardrails. Prompts are versioned in the repo.

Consequences

✓ Keeps per-conversation cost within budget and first-token latency low for the common case.
✓ Grounding + citations improve trust and reduce hallucination risk.
– Router adds complexity and a misclassification failure mode (a hard query sent to the small tier).
– Accepted tradeoff (59.2): we optimize Cost + Latency for the common path and buy Quality only where routing detects it's needed. Mitigation: monitor escalation accuracy; alert on rising deflection failures.

Alternatives considered

Single large tier for everything — higher quality everywhere but over budget and slower for simple FAQs. Single small tier for everything — cheapest/fastest but under-serves complex cases and risks wrong answers. Routing was chosen to balance both.

💡 Tips & Tricks
  • Mnemonic — "C.D.C.A.": Context → Decision → Consequences → Alternatives. Miss "Consequences" and it's a decision announcement, not a record.
  • One decision per ADR — keep them short (often < 1 page) and numbered sequentially.
  • Immutable: Don't edit an accepted ADR to reverse it — write a new one and mark the old Superseded. The history is the value.
  • Write "We will…" in active voice and store ADRs in the repo (/docs/adr/) next to the code they govern.
  • For AI ADRs, always include: model tier, eval/acceptance criteria, cost & latency budget, safety controls, and fallback behavior.
⚠️ Common Pitfalls
  • Omitting negative consequences: An ADR with only upsides isn't honest and hides the accepted tradeoff.
  • Bundling many decisions: "Our whole AI architecture" in one ADR becomes unmaintainable — split it.
  • Editing history: Rewriting an accepted ADR destroys the audit trail. Supersede instead.
  • No status / no number: Un-numbered, statusless notes aren't ADRs — you lose ordering and lifecycle.
🎯 Exam Strategy — 59.4

Likely question types: "Which field is missing from this ADR?", ordering the ADR sections, "how do you change an accepted decision?" (answer: supersede), identifying AI-specific fields.

Answer structure: Name the decision → give Context (forces) → state Decision (active voice) → list Consequences (good + bad) → note Alternatives → set Status.

Graders look for: "one decision per record", "immutable / superseded", "context, decision, consequences, alternatives", "status", and AI extensions (model tier, evals, cost/latency budget, guardrails, fallback).

59.5

Hands-On: Writing an Architecture Decision Presentation

This section combines 59.1–59.4 into a single deliverable: a short, persuasive presentation that gets a decision approved. The goal is a decision, not a lecture — lead with the recommendation, make the tradeoff explicit, and set honest expectations.

Step-by-step template

  1. Define the decision & audience. One sentence: "We need to decide X for audience Y." Rank the audience's priorities using CLASS (Cost, Latency, Accuracy, Safety, Scale).
  2. State the recommendation up front (BLUF). Slide 1 gives the answer and the single most important reason.
  3. Give just enough context. The forces at play — volume, stakes, constraints, budget — at the audience's abstraction rung (59.1).
  4. Present the options as a tradeoff. 2–3 real options in a matrix across Cost / Quality / Latency / Safety (59.2). Name what each sacrifices.
  5. Set realistic expectations. Explicitly state what the system will and won't guarantee, and the mitigations (59.3).
  6. Show the decision & consequences. The chosen option, the accepted tradeoff, and how you'll measure success (evals, SLOs, monitoring).
  7. Define the ask & next steps. What you need approved (budget, headcount, timeline) and what happens after "yes".
  8. Attach the ADR. The presentation persuades; the ADR (59.4) records. Reference the ADR number.

Sample mini-presentation outline (worked example)

Decision: which model strategy to fund for the Customer Support Bot — audience: VP of Support (non-technical) + Eng Lead.

Recommendation (BLUF)

"Fund a two-tier support assistant: fast/cheap model for common questions, escalate hard ones. Grounded in our help center, with citations. Expected: faster replies within a capped per-chat cost."

Why now / Context

~8,000 chats/day, mostly routine FAQs; agents overloaded on repetitive questions. Budget and speed targets stated in business terms.

The options (tradeoff matrix)

Option A: one big model (best quality, over budget/slow). Option B: one small model (cheapest/fastest, weaker on hard cases). Option C: two-tier routing (balances Cost/Latency with Quality). Show what each sacrifices.

What it will & won't do

Drafts grounded answers with sources; makes agents faster. Will not auto-close tickets or give regulated advice; occasional errors are possible, so answers are assistive and cited (59.3).

Decision & how we'll measure it

Choose Option C. Accepted tradeoff: optimize cost/latency on the common path, buy quality only where routing detects complexity. Success = deflection rate, escalation accuracy, cost/chat, CSAT — tracked on a dashboard.

The ask & next steps

Approve budget + 3-week pilot on the top 20 FAQ intents. On success, expand. Reference ADR-014 for the full record.

Self-review checklist (before "submission")

  • Does slide 1 state the recommendation (BLUF), not build up to it?
  • Is the language at the audience's abstraction rung — no unexplained jargon (59.1)?
  • Are there 2–3 real options shown as a tradeoff matrix, each with a named sacrifice (59.2)?
  • Did I quantify cost/latency/quality targets instead of using adjectives?
  • Did I set honest expectations — what it won't do, and the mitigations (59.3)?
  • Is there a measurement plan (evals / SLOs / monitoring) for the chosen option?
  • Is the ask explicit (budget, timeline, scope) with clear next steps?
  • Is a numbered ADR attached with Context, Decision, Consequences, Alternatives (59.4)?
  • Would a skeptical CFO and a skeptical Eng Lead both find it credible?
💡 Tips & Tricks
  • Mnemonic — "R.O.T.E.D." presentation spine: Recommendation → Options (tradeoffs) → Truth (expectations) → Evidence (metrics/evals) → Decision + ask.
  • One slide = one idea. If a slide needs a paragraph to explain, it's two slides.
  • Put backup detail in an appendix — architecture diagrams and benchmarks live there, ready if asked, out of the main flow.
  • Rehearse the objection: pre-answer "why not just the cheap model?" and "can it be 100% accurate?" on their own slides.
⚠️ Common Pitfalls
  • Lecture, not decision: 20 slides of how it works and no clear ask.
  • Only presenting the chosen option: Without alternatives, stakeholders can't trust the choice was reasoned.
  • Burying the tradeoff and the ask on the last slide when time runs out.
  • No measurement plan — "we'll know it's working" is not a metric.
🎯 Exam Strategy — 59.5

Likely question types: "Order these presentation sections", "what's missing from this decision deck?", short-answer: outline a decision presentation for scenario X.

Answer structure: Recommendation (BLUF) → context → options as tradeoffs → expectations/limits → decision + metrics → the ask → attach ADR.

Graders look for: "BLUF / recommendation first", "options + tradeoff matrix", "named sacrifice", "realistic expectations", "measurement / evals / SLOs", "explicit ask", "ADR attached".

Test

Self-Test — 5 Sample Questions

Attempt each before expanding the answer. Explanations note the keywords graders reward.

Q1(Scenario) A VP of Marketing asks how your new content-drafting assistant works. Which opening is best?
Show answer

Best: "It drafts first-version copy from your brief — like a fast junior writer that always needs an editor's review." (Leads with outcome + analogy + honest boundary — 59.1 & 59.3.)

Wrong: "It uses a transformer to autoregressively generate tokens conditioned on your prompt embedding." (Jargon-first; ignores audience.) Also wrong: "It writes perfect final copy automatically." (Over-promises — violates 59.3.)

Keywords: audience-appropriate, outcome-first, analogy, requires validation.

Q2(MCQ) A high-volume, low-stakes text-tagging job runs 2M items/day. Which optimization is most appropriate?
A) Largest model tier for max quality   B) Smaller/faster tier + caching + batching   C) Add human review to every item   D) Maximize output length
Show answer

Answer: B. High volume + low stakes → optimize Cost + Latency with a smaller tier, prompt caching, and batch processing. A over-spends; C is safety overkill that destroys throughput; D increases cost for no benefit.

Keywords: optimize per use case, no free lunch, cost/latency levers, scale.

Q3(Short answer) A stakeholder insists the compliance-review assistant must be "100% accurate and fully automated." How do you respond?
Show answer

Model answer: Explain that no LLM configuration guarantees 100% accuracy — outputs can occasionally be confidently wrong (hallucination) and are non-deterministic. Reframe it as high-confidence assistance requiring human validation: the assistant pre-screens and flags issues with citations to the exact policy clause, a human makes the final call, and we measure flag accuracy against a labeled eval set and log every recommendation for audit. This delivers most of the speed while keeping the regulated decision human-owned.

Keywords: no 100% guarantee, hallucination, human-in-the-loop, evals/acceptance threshold, citations/grounding, audit trail.

Q4(MCQ) An accepted ADR's decision needs to be reversed after six months. What's the correct action?
A) Edit the ADR text to the new decision   B) Delete the ADR   C) Write a new ADR and mark the old one "Superseded"   D) Add a comment in the code only
Show answer

Answer: C. ADRs are immutable / append-only. Preserve history by writing a new numbered ADR and marking the previous one Superseded (with a link). Editing or deleting destroys the audit trail — the whole point of ADRs.

Keywords: immutable, supersede (don't edit/delete), status, audit trail.

Q5(Scenario) Review this decision deck: 15 slides of architecture diagrams, one option shown, no metrics, the budget ask on the final slide. Name three fixes.
Show answer
