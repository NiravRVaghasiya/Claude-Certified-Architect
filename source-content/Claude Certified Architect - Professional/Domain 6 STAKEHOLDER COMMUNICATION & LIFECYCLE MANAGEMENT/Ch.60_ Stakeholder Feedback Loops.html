60.1 — Managing Feedback Loops Across the Development Lifecycle

Core Concept Summary

A feedback loop is any recurring cycle in which work is produced, reviewed by those who will use or fund it, and adjusted based on their response. In a delivery engagement, feedback loops are the primary mechanism that keeps a build aligned with real needs rather than with assumptions made at kickoff. An architect's job is not only to design the system but to design the cadence and channels through which stakeholders shape it over time.

Feedback loops exist at multiple tempos, and mature teams run several at once. Fast, tight loops (automated tests, code review, CI checks) give the delivery team confidence within minutes to hours. Medium loops (sprint reviews, demos, usability sessions) surface product-fit issues within days to weeks. Slow loops (steering committees, quarterly business reviews, production telemetry) validate business outcomes over weeks to months. The core principle from Lean and Agile is the same: the shorter the loop, the cheaper the correction. A misunderstanding caught in a demo costs a conversation; the same misunderstanding caught after launch costs a rebuild.

Effective loops share three properties. They are timely (feedback arrives while the work is still cheap to change), specific (tied to a concrete artifact — a demo, a prototype, a metric — not a vague status), and closed (the team visibly acts on input and reports back what changed, so stakeholders learn that participating is worth their time). A loop that gathers feedback but never demonstrably acts on it decays quickly: stakeholders stop engaging, and the project drifts back toward assumption-driven delivery.

Across the lifecycle — Discovery, Design, Build, Validate, Deploy, Operate — the question each loop answers changes. Early loops ask "are we building the right thing?" (validation of problem and scope). Later loops ask "are we building the thing right?" (verification of quality and performance). An architect maps which stakeholders own which question at each stage, so the right people give the right feedback at the point where it still has leverage.

Key Terms & Definitions

Feedback Loop
A recurring cycle of produce → observe → interpret → adjust that steers work toward intended outcomes.
Loop Latency
The elapsed time from producing something to receiving actionable feedback on it. Lower latency = cheaper corrections.
Validation vs. Verification
Validation = "did we build the right thing?" (fit to need). Verification = "did we build the thing right?" (fit to spec/quality).
Closing the Loop
Visibly acting on feedback and reporting the resulting change back to the contributor.
Feedback Debt
Accumulated, un-actioned or un-solicited feedback that grows more expensive to resolve over time.
Cadence
The fixed, predictable rhythm (e.g., biweekly demo) at which a loop repeats.

Tips & Tricks

  • Run loops at multiple tempos simultaneously — never rely on a single monthly meeting to catch everything.
  • Mnemonic TSC: every good loop is Timely, Specific, Closed.
  • Always close the loop out loud. Start each demo with "here's what changed based on your last feedback" — this trains stakeholders that engagement pays off.
  • Attach feedback to an artifact. A clickable prototype elicits far better input than a status slide.
  • Front-load validation loops. The "right thing?" question loses leverage after Build starts.
⚠️ Common Pitfalls
  • Big-bang feedback: saving all review for a single end-of-project sign-off, guaranteeing expensive late rework.
  • Open loops: collecting feedback but never reporting back what was done — stakeholders disengage.
  • Wrong audience, wrong stage: asking executives for pixel-level UI feedback, or asking end-users to validate business ROI.
  • Vanity demos: showing only happy paths so no real feedback is provoked.
  • Confusing verification for validation: a bug-free build of the wrong feature still fails the stakeholder.
🎯 Exam Strategy This topic is tested with scenario questions where a project surfaces a problem late. The correct answer almost always favors shorter, earlier, more frequent feedback loops over a single large review. Watch for the validation-vs-verification distinction: if the scenario describes building the wrong thing, the fix is an earlier validation loop, not more testing. Distractors will tempt you with "add more QA" or "write a more detailed spec up front" — both address verification or assumptions, not the loop latency the scenario actually reveals.

60.2 — SLA Definition and Expectation Alignment

Core Concept Summary

A Service Level Agreement (SLA) is a written, measurable commitment about the level of service a stakeholder can expect — availability, response time, resolution time, throughput, or quality. Its purpose is to convert fuzzy, subjective expectations ("it should be fast," "someone should always be reachable") into objective, time-bound, and testable targets that both sides agree to before delivery begins. Expectation alignment is the broader discipline; the SLA is its most formal artifact.

A robust SLA rests on well-defined Service Level Indicators (SLIs) — the actual measurements (e.g., "95th-percentile API latency") — and Service Level Objectives (SLOs) — the internal target you engineer toward (e.g., "p95 latency under 300 ms"). The SLA is the externally committed promise, usually set slightly looser than the SLO so the team has headroom before breaching a contract. The gap between "how good we build it" (SLO) and "what we promise" (SLA) is deliberate risk management, not sloppiness.

Alignment also means being explicit about what is out of scope: exclusions, maintenance windows, dependencies on the client's own systems, and the consequences of a breach (credits, escalation, remediation). Equally important is defining the measurement method and reporting cadence — an SLA nobody measures or reports is just a hope. Good practice ties every SLA target to the SMART discipline: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound.

The most common alignment failure is not a missed metric — it is an unstated assumption. When a stakeholder says "real-time," the architect must translate that into a number, confirm it in writing, and note what is excluded. The SLA conversation is where informal, verbal expectations become durable, shared commitments; skipping it merely defers the disagreement to a moment when it is far more expensive.

Key Terms & Definitions

SLA (Service Level Agreement)
The externally committed, measurable promise about service levels, including consequences for breach.
SLO (Service Level Objective)
The internal target the team engineers toward — usually stricter than the SLA to preserve headroom.
SLI (Service Level Indicator)
The concrete metric that is measured (e.g., uptime %, p95 latency, resolution time).
Error Budget
The allowable amount of failure within an SLO (e.g., 0.1% downtime); consumed by incidents, guides release risk.
SMART Target
Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound — the quality test for any committed number.
Escalation Path
The predefined sequence of who is notified, and when, as a breach or severity increases.
Exclusion / Maintenance Window
Explicitly agreed periods or conditions during which SLA targets do not apply.

Tips & Tricks

  • Set the SLO stricter than the SLA. The buffer is your safety margin against contractual breach.
  • Separate response from resolution. "We'll acknowledge in 15 min" is not "we'll fix in 15 min." Commit to both.
  • Translate every adjective into a number. "Fast," "reliable," "real-time" are not commitments until quantified.
  • Write down the exclusions too. Maintenance windows and client-side dependencies are part of the agreement.
  • Mnemonic SMART for every target: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound.
⚠️ Common Pitfalls
  • Verbal SLAs: "we agreed you'd be reachable" — unwritten, unmeasured, unenforceable.
  • Unachievable promises: committing to 99.999% on infrastructure that cannot deliver it, to win approval.
  • No measurement method: an SLA with no agreed metric, tool, or reporting cadence.
  • Ignoring dependencies: promising uptime that depends on a third party or the client's own network.
  • Conflating SLO and SLA: committing your internal stretch goal as the external promise, leaving no headroom.
🎯 Exam Strategy SLA questions are usually scenario-based with a vague client expectation ("the client wants it to be always available"). The best answer emphasizes written, measurable, time-bound commitments over informal verbal ones, and separates response from resolution. A frequent distractor offers an impressively high number (e.g., "commit to 100% uptime") — reject it: unachievable targets are a trap, and 100% is never a defensible SLA. Also watch for answers that skip defining how the metric is measured; an unmeasurable SLA is not the correct choice.

60.3 — Handling Stakeholder Pushback and Scope Creep

Core Concept Summary

Pushback is any stakeholder resistance — to a decision, a timeline, a trade-off, or a "no." Scope creep is the uncontrolled expansion of what the project must deliver, typically through a steady accumulation of small, individually reasonable requests that were never re-baselined against time, cost, and risk. The two are linked: unmanaged pushback frequently resolves into silent scope creep, because saying "yes" in the moment feels easier than negotiating the trade-off.

The professional response is not to refuse changes — requirements legitimately evolve, and rigidly rejecting them is its own failure mode. The response is to make every change visible, assessed, and decided through a lightweight change-control process. When a new request arrives, it is logged in a scope-change register, its impact on schedule, cost, and risk is assessed, and a decision-maker (often via a Change Control Board or the product owner) explicitly accepts or defers it — usually with a corresponding trade-off ("yes, and we drop X" or "yes, and the date moves"). This preserves responsiveness while preventing invisible drift.

Handling pushback well is largely about separating the person from the problem and grounding the conversation in the shared, previously agreed baseline (the scope statement, the SLA, the prioritized backlog). Techniques from principled negotiation apply: acknowledge the underlying interest behind the request, restate the agreed constraints, and present the trade-off as a joint decision rather than a refusal. The iron triangle (scope, time, cost — with quality at the center) is the architect's most powerful framing tool: you cannot expand one corner without moving another, and making that visible turns an argument into a prioritization choice.

Authority matters here. A RACI matrix clarifies who is Accountable for scope decisions so that pushback is routed to the person empowered to decide, not absorbed silently by whoever received the request. When everyone knows who owns the trade-off, "no" and "not now" become organizational decisions rather than personal confrontations.

Key Terms & Definitions

Scope Creep
Uncontrolled growth of deliverables without corresponding adjustment to time, cost, or resources.
Gold-Plating
The team adding unrequested "extras" it thinks the client will like — a self-inflicted form of scope creep.
Change-Control Process
The defined workflow for logging, assessing, and approving/deferring changes to baselined scope.
Scope-Change Register
The living log of every requested change, its impact assessment, and its decision.
Change Control Board (CCB)
The group/role empowered to approve or reject scope changes.
Iron Triangle
The scope–time–cost constraint (quality at center); moving one corner forces another to move.
RACI
Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed — clarifies decision ownership. Exactly one "A" per decision.
Baseline
The formally agreed reference version of scope/schedule/cost against which changes are measured.

Tips & Tricks

  • Never say a flat "no." Say "yes — and here's the trade-off." Frame it as a joint prioritization decision.
  • Log first, decide second. Capturing a request in the register is not a commitment; it defuses the moment and buys assessment time.
  • Anchor to the baseline. Return every debate to the agreed scope statement, SLA, or prioritized backlog.
  • Attack the problem, not the person — acknowledge the interest behind the request before discussing constraints.
  • Route to the "A". Use RACI so the trade-off is decided by whoever is Accountable, not absorbed by whoever was asked.
  • Guard against gold-plating — your own team can create scope creep by over-delivering.
⚠️ Common Pitfalls
  • Silent yes: agreeing to a mid-sprint addition without logging it or moving the date — the classic creep vector.
  • Rigid no: refusing all change; legitimate evolution gets blocked and the relationship sours.
  • No single owner: unclear accountability means requests are absorbed by whoever is closest, not decided.
  • Skipping impact assessment: approving changes without quantifying the schedule/cost/risk cost.
  • Personalizing pushback: treating disagreement as conflict rather than an unresolved trade-off.
🎯 Exam Strategy Expect a "stakeholder requests an unplanned feature mid-sprint / mid-project" scenario. The best first response is almost always to log the request and assess its impact before committing — not to immediately accept it (creep) and not to flatly reject it (rigidity). Distractors are engineered to feel accommodating ("just add it, the client is important") or decisive ("refuse, it's out of scope"). Both skip the change-control step. If the scenario involves unclear decision authority, the answer points to RACI / the accountable owner. Remember: the exam rewards process and visibility, not heroics.

60.4 — Iteration Communication: Progress Reports and Demos

Core Concept Summary

Iteration communication is how the team keeps stakeholders continuously informed of progress, risks, and decisions between formal milestones. Its two workhorses are the progress report (a concise, regular written status) and the demo (a live showing of working increments). Together they replace anxiety-driven "what's the status?" interruptions with a predictable, trusted rhythm — which is itself a form of expectation management.

Effective progress reports are tailored to the audience. Executives and sponsors want outcomes, risks, and decisions needed from them — typically a RAG status (Red / Amber / Green), key metrics, and a short "asks" section. Delivery-level stakeholders want detail: what shipped, what's next, what's blocked. A single report that tries to serve both usually serves neither. The reliable structure is Progress → Plan → Problems (blockers) → Asks: what we did, what we'll do next, what's in our way, and what we need from you.

Demos are the highest-bandwidth loop because they show working software, not descriptions of it — which is why Agile treats "working software" as the primary measure of progress. A good demo shows a real, integrated increment against the acceptance criteria the stakeholders agreed to, invites reaction, and captures feedback into the backlog on the spot. It is emphatically not a slideware status meeting. Crucially, a demo should surface problems, not hide them: showing an honest partial result early is worth more than a polished illusion of completeness.

Cadence is the quiet superpower of iteration communication. A predictable rhythm — a report every Friday, a demo every second Thursday — builds trust because stakeholders learn they will be informed without having to chase. Predictability also protects the team: it channels feedback into scheduled forums rather than a constant stream of ad-hoc interruptions. The architect's job is to design a cadence proportional to project risk and stakeholder anxiety, and then to honor it relentlessly, especially when the news is bad.

Key Terms & Definitions

RAG Status
Red/Amber/Green health indicator summarizing whether an item is on track, at risk, or in trouble.
Progress → Plan → Problems (3P)
A concise report structure: what was done, what's next, what's blocking, plus explicit asks.
Sprint Review / Demo
A recurring session showing the working increment to stakeholders and capturing feedback.
Definition of Done (DoD)
The shared checklist an increment must meet to be considered complete and demo-worthy.
Acceptance Criteria
The specific, testable conditions a feature must satisfy to be accepted by the stakeholder.
Cadence
The fixed rhythm of reports and demos; predictability builds trust and controls interruptions.
Burndown / Burnup
Charts showing remaining vs. completed work over time — a common progress visual.

Tips & Tricks

  • Tailor by altitude. Give executives outcomes and asks; give the team detail. Never one report for all.
  • Mnemonic 3P + Asks: Progress, Plan, Problems, and what you need from them.
  • Demo working software, not slides. Show a real increment against agreed acceptance criteria.
  • Honor the cadence — especially with bad news. Predictability builds trust; a skipped report reads as trouble.
  • Capture feedback live. Log demo reactions straight into the backlog so the loop visibly closes.
  • Lead reports with the RAG and the asks — busy stakeholders read the top, not the appendix.
⚠️ Common Pitfalls
  • Watermelon status: reporting "Green" outside while the project is "Red" inside — trust collapses when it surfaces.
  • Demo theater: showing mock-ups or happy-path-only builds that provoke no real feedback.
  • Firehose reports: dumping raw task detail on executives who need outcomes and decisions.
  • Irregular cadence: reporting only when convenient; silence during bad periods erodes confidence.
  • No asks: reports that inform but never state what the team needs from the stakeholder.
🎯 Exam Strategy Iteration-communication questions test audience tailoring and honesty. When a scenario describes an executive overwhelmed by detail, the answer is a summarized, outcome-focused report (RAG + asks), not "send more information." When a scenario reveals a project is behind, the answer favors transparent, timely disclosure through the normal cadence over delaying or sugar-coating — the "watermelon" report is always the wrong choice. Distractors will offer polished, reassuring options that hide risk; the defensible answer surfaces the problem early. Also know that Agile treats working software as the truest progress signal, so "show a demo of the working increment" beats "present a status deck."

60.5 — Hands-On: Designing a Stakeholder Communication Plan

Core Concept Summary

A stakeholder communication plan is a single, deliberate artifact that answers, for every stakeholder: who needs what information, why, in what format, through which channel, at what cadence, and who is responsible for producing it. It operationalizes everything in this chapter — feedback loops, SLAs, change control, and iteration reporting — into one durable, agreed reference. It is not documentation for its own sake; it is the instrument that prevents the two most common relationship failures: the important stakeholder who feels ignored, and the busy stakeholder who feels spammed.

Building one follows a repeatable sequence. First, identify and analyze stakeholders: list everyone affected and classify them, most commonly on a power/interest grid (high power + high interest → Manage Closely; high power + low interest → Keep Satisfied; low power + high interest → Keep Informed; low power + low interest → Monitor). This classification directly drives cadence and depth: you do not communicate with a peripheral observer the way you do with the funding sponsor. Second, define the information needs and the decision rights for each group, ideally cross-referenced to a RACI so accountability is unambiguous.

Third, choose channel, format, and cadence per stakeholder group, matching altitude (executives → concise RAG; team → detailed board) and matching richness to the message (a routine update can be async email; a difficult trade-off deserves a synchronous conversation). Fourth, assign an owner and a feedback mechanism for each communication, so it is clear who produces it and how the recipient responds — closing the loop by design rather than by luck. Finally, treat the plan as a living document: revisit it when stakeholders, phases, or risk levels change.

The payoff is that expectation management stops being reactive. Instead of discovering after the fact that a VP felt out of the loop, the architect has already decided that this VP is "Manage Closely," receives a monthly one-page RAG with an explicit asks section, owned by the delivery lead, with a standing 20-minute review. The plan turns the soft skill of stakeholder communication into an engineered, auditable system.

Key Terms & Definitions

Communication Plan
The artifact specifying who/what/why/how/when/owner for every stakeholder communication.
Stakeholder Analysis
Identifying and classifying stakeholders to determine engagement strategy.
Power/Interest Grid
A 2×2 classifying stakeholders by influence and interest → Manage Closely / Keep Satisfied / Keep Informed / Monitor.
Channel Richness
How much nuance a medium carries; match rich media (live conversation) to complex/emotional messages.
RACI (in the plan)
Ties each communication and decision to a Responsible producer and one Accountable owner.
Engagement Level
Current vs. desired involvement of a stakeholder (unaware → resistant → neutral → supportive → leading).

Tips & Tricks

  • Classify before you schedule. Cadence and depth follow from the power/interest quadrant, not from habit.
  • One accountable owner per communication. An unowned report is a report that eventually stops going out.
  • Match richness to the message. Async email for routine updates; a live conversation for bad news or trade-offs.
  • Build in the return path. Every communication should say how the recipient gives feedback — design the loop closed.
  • Keep it living. Re-run stakeholder analysis at each phase gate; stakeholders and their interest levels change.
  • Mnemonic — the plan answers "Who / What / Why / How / When / Who-owns-it."
⚠️ Common Pitfalls
  • One-size-fits-all: the same report to sponsor and developer — over-informing one, under-informing the other.
  • Missing owner: communications with no responsible producer quietly lapse.
  • Set-and-forget: writing the plan once and never revising it as the project and its stakeholders evolve.
  • Channel mismatch: delivering sensitive or complex news via a low-richness channel (e.g., a terse email).
  • Ignoring low-power/high-interest voices who are often the actual end users and best source of validation feedback.
🎯 Exam Strategy Expect a "design/choose the communication approach for stakeholder X" scenario. The correct answer is driven by the stakeholder's power/interest classification: a high-power, low-interest executive should be Kept Satisfied with concise, outcome-level updates — not flooded with detail (a common distractor), and not ignored (another distractor). When the scenario involves sensitive or bad news, the defensible choice favors a richer, synchronous channel over a terse written note. Also watch for the "no owner assigned" trap: a plan is only correct when every communication has a clear accountable owner and a defined cadence.

Self-Check Questions answers hidden

Q1. A project's stakeholders only review the product at a single sign-off near the end, and major misalignment is discovered. What is the most effective corrective action?

Answer: Introduce shorter, more frequent feedback loops (e.g., sprint demos) earlier in the lifecycle so misalignment is caught while it is still cheap to fix.

Why: The root problem is loop latency, not effort or documentation. "Add more QA" (verification) or "write a more detailed spec" (up-front assumption) are tempting distractors but do not address the late-validation gap.

Q2. A client says the system must be "always available." How should the architect handle this expectation?

Answer: Translate it into a specific, measurable, time-bound SLA (e.g., 99.9% monthly uptime, with defined maintenance windows and measurement method), agreed in writing.

Why: "Always available" is not a commitment until quantified. Committing to 100% uptime is a distractor — it is unachievable and indefensible. An SLA with no measurement method is also incorrect.

Q3. Midway through a sprint, an important stakeholder requests an unplanned feature. What is the best first response?

Answer: Log the request in the scope-change register and assess its impact on schedule, cost, and risk before committing.

Why: This prevents silent scope creep while staying responsive. "Just add it" (creep) and "refuse, it's out of scope" (rigidity) both skip change control and are the classic distractors.

Q4. What is the relationship between an SLI, an SLO, and an SLA?

Answer: The SLI is the metric you measure; the SLO is the internal target you engineer toward (set stricter); the SLA is the external, committed promise (set looser than the SLO to preserve headroom before a breach).

Why: The deliberate gap between SLO and SLA is risk management. Answers that treat SLO and SLA as identical miss the headroom principle.

Q5. A project is behind schedule. The weekly report is due. What should the delivery lead do?

Answer: Report transparently and on the normal cadence — an honest Amber/Red status with the cause, impact, mitigation, and any asks.

Why: Predictable, honest communication builds trust. Reporting "Green" while the project is troubled (a "watermelon" status) or delaying the report are distractors that erode credibility when the truth surfaces.

Q6. An executive sponsor has high influence but limited day-to-day interest and complains about receiving too much detail. How should they be engaged?

Answer: Treat them as "Keep Satisfied" — provide concise, outcome-focused updates (RAG status + key risks + decisions needed) at a lower cadence, not detailed task-level reports.

Why: The power/interest classification drives the approach. "Send more detail" over-informs; "stop updating them" ignores a high-power stakeholder — both are distractors.

Q7. Two teams keep disputing whether a change is in scope, and no decision gets made. What is missing?

Answer: Clear decision accountability — a RACI with exactly one Accountable owner (e.g., the product owner or Change Control Board) empowered to accept or defer the change.

Why: Unclear ownership leaves pushback absorbed or unresolved. "Escalate every dispute to the sponsor" is a distractor that does not fix the structural gap.

Q8. During a sprint review, the team presents a polished slide deck describing the work instead of showing the running increment. What is the concern?

Answer: Slides describe progress but do not demonstrate it. Agile treats working software as the primary measure of progress, so the review should show a real, integrated increment against agreed acceptance criteria to elicit genuine feedback.

Why: "Demo theater" (slides or happy-path mock-ups) hides risk and provokes no useful feedback — the opposite of a well-designed feedback loop.
