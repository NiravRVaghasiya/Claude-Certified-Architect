58.1Conducting Discovery Sessions with Business Stakeholders

Discovery is the structured, facilitated process of eliciting the real business problem, its context, and its success criteria before any solution is designed. For AI projects, disciplined discovery is the primary defence against building a technically impressive model that solves the wrong problem.

🧭Concept Overview

A discovery session is a facilitated conversation (workshop, interview, or observation) whose goal is to understand the business context — who is affected, what outcome they need, why it matters, and how success will be measured. In AI engagements, discovery must also surface where a probabilistic system is acceptable, what "good enough" accuracy looks like, and how humans stay in the loop. The architect acts as a facilitator and translator, not a note-taker: guiding the conversation, drawing out tacit knowledge, and continuously validating understanding.

📖Key Terminology

Stakeholder
Any person or group who affects, or is affected by, the AI system — sponsors, end users, subject-matter experts (SMEs), data owners, compliance, and those impacted by the model's decisions.
Stakeholder Mapping
Classifying stakeholders by influence and interest to decide how to engage each (typically a power/interest grid).
Elicitation
The active drawing-out of needs, rules, and tacit knowledge — distinct from stakeholders simply "telling you the requirements."
Active Listening
Listening to understand, using paraphrasing, clarifying questions, and reflection to confirm meaning rather than assuming it.
Facilitation
Neutrally steering a group conversation so every voice is heard, scope stays on track, and decisions are captured.
RACI
A responsibility matrix labelling each person Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, or Informed for a task or decision.

🔍Detailed Breakdown

  1. Identify & map stakeholders. List everyone touched by the workflow, then place them on a power/interest grid: high-power/high-interest players are managed closely; high-power/low-interest are kept satisfied; low-power/high-interest are kept informed; low-power/low-interest are monitored. Confirm who is the Accountable sponsor.
  2. Plan the session. Set a clear objective, timebox each topic, circulate an agenda in advance, and choose the format: 1:1 interviews (deep, candid), workshops (cross-functional alignment), or observation/job-shadowing (reveals tacit "how work really happens").
  3. Open & frame. State purpose, ground rules, and scope. Explicitly separate problem exploration from solutioning — no jumping to "let's use an LLM" before the problem is understood.
  4. Facilitate elicitation. Use open questions ("Walk me through how you handle a difficult case today"), probing ("What happens when that fails?"), and the 5 Whys to reach root cause. Draw out edge cases and current pain metrics.
  5. Practise active listening. Paraphrase back ("So if I understand, the goal is to cut triage time from 4 hours to under 30 minutes — is that right?"). This read-back catches misunderstanding immediately.
  6. Capture & validate. Record decisions, assumptions, open questions, and owners. Close by summarising and confirming next steps.

🏢Real-World Example / Mini Case Study

Case Study

Insurance claims triage assistant. An insurer wants to "use AI to speed up claims." During discovery the architect maps stakeholders — the Claims Ops Director (accountable sponsor), senior adjusters (SMEs), frontline agents (end users), and Compliance. In a workshop, agents job-shadowed reveal the real bottleneck isn't decision-making but gathering scattered documents. Using the 5 Whys, the team learns the true goal is reducing average handling time, not auto-approving claims — which would have created a compliance nightmare. Discovery re-scopes the project from "auto-decision engine" to "document summarisation + retrieval assistant with a human approver," a far safer and higher-value target.

💡Tips & Tricks

💡 Facilitation tips
  • Always restate business goals in your own words before documenting — the read-back is your cheapest defect-detector.
  • Interview SMEs individually first, then run a group workshop — private sessions surface concerns people won't raise in front of leadership.
  • Separate problem from solution: park every premature "we should build X" in a visible parking lot.
  • Ask for a concrete example transaction end-to-end — specifics expose edge cases that abstractions hide.

⚠️Common Pitfalls

  • Solutioning too early — anchoring on "use an LLM" before the problem is understood.
  • Talking only to the sponsor — missing the end users and SMEs who hold tacit knowledge.
  • Leading questions ("You'd want it automated, right?") that plant answers instead of eliciting them.
  • No validation loop — leaving the room without confirming shared understanding.
  • Ignoring the impacted (non-user) parties whose jobs or customers the model affects.

🎯Exam Strategy

Expect scenario questions that describe a poorly-run kickoff and ask what the architect should do next. Watch keywords: "jumped straight to a model" (→ pitfall: premature solutioning), "only the VP was consulted" (→ incomplete stakeholder mapping), "confirm understanding" (→ active listening / read-back). Best-answer logic: understand the problem and validate it before designing a solution. When two answers seem right, prefer the one that engages more stakeholders or validates understanding over the one that starts building.

58.2Translating Business Language into Technical Requirements

Business stakeholders speak in outcomes ("reduce churn," "answer customers faster"). Architects must convert those into precise, testable technical requirements without losing intent or introducing ambiguity — and keep every requirement traceable back to its business driver.

🧭Concept Overview

Translation is a bidirectional mapping: each business need becomes one or more technical requirements, and each technical requirement can be traced back to the business goal that justifies it. The architect decomposes a vague objective into capabilities, then into functional and non-functional requirements, attaching measurable acceptance criteria. In AI projects this includes specifying the task type (classification, extraction, generation, ranking), the quality bar (accuracy/precision/recall or human-eval rubric), and the failure behaviour.

📖Key Terminology

Functional Requirement (FR)
What the system must do — a behaviour or capability (e.g. "classify each email into one of five intents").
Non-Functional Requirement (NFR)
A quality attribute or constraint — how well it must perform (latency, accuracy, availability, security, cost).
Acceptance Criteria
Objective, testable conditions that determine when a requirement is satisfied.
Requirement Traceability
An explicit link from each requirement to its business driver and to the test that verifies it.
Traceability Matrix (RTM)
A table mapping business need → requirement → design element → test case.
Ambiguity
Language open to multiple interpretations ("fast," "accurate," "user-friendly") — the enemy of a testable requirement.

🔍Detailed Breakdown

  1. Decompose the goal. Break "improve support" into capabilities: auto-categorise tickets, draft replies, surface knowledge-base articles.
  2. Classify each capability into functional (behaviour) vs non-functional (quality) requirements.
  3. Quantify vague terms. Replace "fast" with "P95 response < 2 s"; replace "accurate" with "≥ 90% top-1 intent accuracy on the held-out set."
  4. Specify AI-specific attributes — task type, quality metric & threshold, acceptable error modes, human-in-the-loop points, data freshness, and explainability needs.
  5. Attach acceptance criteria to every requirement so it is testable.
  6. Record traceability in an RTM so every requirement can be justified and later verified.

🏢Real-World Example / Mini Case Study

Case Study

Retail chatbot. Business asks for "a smart bot that understands customers." The architect decomposes this into FRs (detect intent, retrieve order status, escalate to human on low confidence) and NFRs (P95 < 1.5 s, ≥ 88% intent accuracy, English + Polish, no order data in logs). Each is entered in an RTM linked to the business driver "reduce contact-centre volume by 20%." Later, when a developer proposes caching responses for speed, the RTM immediately flags that this could violate the "real-time order status" FR — traceability caught a conflict before it shipped.

💡Tips & Tricks

💡 Translation tips
  • Every requirement needs a number or a testable condition — if you can't test it, it isn't a requirement yet.
  • Write NFRs as metric + threshold + condition (e.g. "accuracy ≥ 90% on production-representative data").
  • Maintain a shared glossary so "ticket," "case," and "claim" mean the same thing to everyone.
  • Keep the RTM alive — it's your fastest tool to assess the impact of any scope change.

⚠️Common Pitfalls

  • Leaving vague adjectives ("fast," "robust," "intelligent") unquantified.
  • Confusing FRs with NFRs — "must be accurate" is a quality attribute (NFR), not a behaviour.
  • Gold-plating — inventing technical requirements no stakeholder asked for.
  • No traceability — orphan requirements that can't be justified or tested.
  • Setting an accuracy target with no defined evaluation set — a metric without a test is meaningless.

🎯Exam Strategy

Common question style: "Which of these is a well-formed requirement?" — the correct answer is specific, measurable, and testable; distractors contain vague words. Also expect FR-vs-NFR classification items: remember FR = what it does, NFR = how well / under what constraint. If asked what to do about a vague business ask, the best answer is usually quantify it and confirm the acceptance criteria with stakeholders, not "start building."

58.3Identifying Constraints: Budget, Timeline, Compliance & Data Availability

Constraints are the boundaries within which a solution must fit. Surfacing them early turns invisible risk into explicit, manageable decisions — and in AI projects, data availability and compliance are frequently the binding constraints, not compute.

🧭Concept Overview

A constraint is any limitation on the solution space — money, time, regulation, data, technology, or organisation. Discovery must categorise constraints, assess their impact and likelihood, and analyse the trade-offs between them (the classic "scope–time–cost" triangle, plus quality). For AI specifically, the decisive question is often "do we have enough representative, permitted, labelled data?" — a data constraint can invalidate an entire approach regardless of budget.

📖Key Terminology

Constraint
A fixed limit the solution must respect (e.g. "€200k cap," "GDPR," "launch by Q3").
Assumption
Something believed true but not yet verified — an unvalidated assumption is a latent risk.
Risk Flagging
Explicitly logging a threat with its likelihood, impact, owner, and mitigation.
Trade-off Analysis
Choosing deliberately between competing constraints (e.g. accept lower scope to hit the deadline).
Data Availability
Whether sufficient, representative, high-quality, legally-usable data exists for the AI task.
Compliance Constraint
A legal/regulatory rule the system must obey — GDPR, HIPAA, EU AI Act, data residency, auditability.

🔍Detailed Breakdown

  • Budget — build vs API cost, inference cost per request at scale, human-review labour, ongoing monitoring/retraining.
  • Timeline — hard external deadlines (regulatory, contractual, seasonal) vs soft internal ones; dependency lead times.
  • Compliance & ethics — data-protection law, residency, right-to-explanation, bias/fairness obligations, audit logging.
  • Data availability & quality — volume, representativeness, labelling, freshness, licensing/consent to use, PII exposure.
  • Technology / organisation — existing stack, security posture, team skills, integration limits.

Process: (1) enumerate constraints per category → (2) rate impact × likelihood → (3) flag the binding constraints → (4) perform trade-off analysis → (5) document as explicit assumptions/risks with owners.

🏢Real-World Example / Mini Case Study

Case Study

Healthcare document classifier. A hospital wants to auto-tag patient records. Budget and timeline look fine — but discovery reveals two binding constraints: records contain PHI governed by HIPAA (compliance), and only 300 labelled examples exist for rare categories (data availability). Trade-off analysis leads the team to (a) process data inside the hospital's private environment with full audit logging, and (b) use a retrieval-augmented, few-shot approach rather than fine-tuning, since the data volume can't support training. The binding constraints — not compute — dictated the architecture.

💡Tips & Tricks

💡 Constraint tips
  • Ask "what's fixed and what's flexible?" for each of scope, time, cost, and quality — you can't fix all four.
  • Validate data availability and compliance earliest — they most often kill or reshape AI projects.
  • Turn every assumption into a written line item with an owner and a date to verify it.
  • Quantify constraints: "€150k," "by 30 June," "≤ 500 ms" — a constraint without a number is just a worry.

⚠️Common Pitfalls

  • Treating compute as the main constraint while ignoring data and legal limits.
  • Leaving assumptions unstated until they fail late in the project.
  • Discovering compliance requirements after building — the most expensive rework of all.
  • Promising all of scope, time, cost, and quality without acknowledging trade-offs.
  • Assuming data is usable without checking consent, licensing, or representativeness.

🎯Exam Strategy

Scenario items will bury the binding constraint in the text — read for keywords: "HIPAA / GDPR / regulated" (compliance), "only a few hundred examples" (data availability), "must launch by" (timeline), "fixed budget of" (budget). The best answer usually validates the constraint before committing to an approach and chooses the technique that fits the constraint (e.g. few-shot/RAG when data is scarce; private hosting when data can't leave). Beware answers that solve a non-binding constraint.

58.4Writing Effective Requirements Documents for AI Projects

A requirements document is the durable, shared contract of what will be built and how success is judged. Good ones are structured, measurable (SMART), and version-controlled so change is traceable.

🧭Concept Overview

The requirements document (often an SRS-style artifact or a Business/Solution Requirements Document) consolidates functional and non-functional requirements, scope, constraints, assumptions, success metrics, and acceptance criteria. It removes ambiguity, aligns stakeholders, and provides the baseline against which delivery is tested. For AI it must additionally cover data requirements, model quality metrics & thresholds, evaluation strategy, human oversight, and responsible-AI/ethical constraints.

📖Key Terminology

SRS
Software Requirements Specification — a structured document of functional + non-functional requirements.
SMART Criteria
Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound — the test for a well-written requirement/objective.
Scope Statement
Explicit in-scope and out-of-scope boundaries — the latter prevents scope creep.
Versioning
Tracking document revisions (v1.0, v1.1…) with a change log, date, author, and rationale.
Baseline
An approved, frozen version used as the reference point for measuring change.
Success Metric / KPI
The measurable business outcome the project must move (e.g. "-20% handling time").

🔍Detailed Breakdown — recommended structure

SectionContents
1. Overview & PurposeBusiness problem, objectives, success metrics/KPIs.
2. Stakeholders & GlossaryRoles (RACI), shared terminology to remove ambiguity.
3. ScopeIn-scope, explicitly out-of-scope, assumptions.
4. Functional RequirementsNumbered behaviours, each with acceptance criteria.
5. Non-Functional RequirementsLatency, accuracy, availability, security, cost, scalability.
6. Data RequirementsSources, volume, labelling, quality, privacy, retention.
7. AI Quality & EvaluationMetrics + thresholds, eval set, human review, monitoring.
8. Constraints & ComplianceBudget, timeline, regulatory, responsible-AI obligations.
9. Traceability MatrixRequirement → business driver → test case.
10. Version HistoryChange log with dates, authors, rationale.

🏢Real-World Example / Mini Case Study

Case Study

Loan-application assistant. The team writes an SRS where FR-04 states: "The system shall generate an applicant summary; all outputs shall be reviewed by a loan officer before a decision (human-in-the-loop)." NFR-07 sets "≥ 95% extraction accuracy on income fields, evaluated on the labelled audit set." Compliance section cites the EU AI Act obligation for human oversight of high-risk decisions. When Legal later requests an explainability log, the change is captured as v1.2 with rationale — and the traceability matrix instantly shows which tests must be updated. The document's structure and versioning kept a regulated project auditable.

💡Tips & Tricks

💡 Documentation tips
  • Use "shall" for mandatory requirements and "should" for desirable ones — consistently.
  • Give every requirement a unique ID (FR-01, NFR-03) so it's referenceable in tests and change logs.
  • Always include an explicit out-of-scope list — it prevents scope creep as effectively as the in-scope list.
  • Pair each requirement with its acceptance test in the same row of the RTM.

⚠️Common Pitfalls

  • Unmeasurable requirements that fail the SMART test.
  • No versioning / change log — nobody knows which version was approved.
  • Missing out-of-scope section, inviting scope creep.
  • Omitting data & evaluation requirements — unique and critical to AI projects.
  • Mixing requirements with design — the doc states what, not the implementation how.

🎯Exam Strategy

Look for the SMART acronym and "which requirement is well-written?" items — pick the specific, measurable, time-bound option. Questions on versioning/baseline test whether you know an approved baseline is the reference for change control. AI-flavoured items add data and evaluation sections — a document missing these is the "incomplete" answer. Remember: requirements say what, not how.

58.5Hands-On: Running a Discovery Session

A practical, step-by-step walkthrough of a discovery session for an AI project — from preparation to a documented output artifact — that ties together everything in 58.1–58.4.

🧭Concept Overview

This is the applied capstone: given a fuzzy business ask, the architect prepares, facilitates, elicits, captures constraints, and produces a discovery summary artifact that seeds the requirements document. The exercise below uses a running scenario — "We want AI to help our support team."

📖Key Terminology

Discovery Summary
The output artifact: problem statement, goals/KPIs, stakeholders, constraints, assumptions, open questions, next steps.
Problem Statement
One or two sentences capturing the real problem, its impact, and the desired outcome.
Parking Lot
A visible list where premature solutions and off-topic items are stored to revisit later.
Open Question / Action Item
An unresolved point with an owner and a due date, carried out of the session.

🔍Detailed Breakdown — the step-by-step run

#StepWhat the architect does
1PrepareMap stakeholders (power/interest), draft objective & agenda, send pre-reads, book the right participants.
2Open & frameState purpose, ground rules, timebox; agree that problem exploration precedes solutioning.
3Explore the problemOpen questions + 5 Whys to reach the root problem and quantify today's pain.
4Define successAgree measurable KPIs and what "good enough" means for an AI system.
5Surface constraintsProbe budget, timeline, compliance, and data availability; log assumptions/risks.
6Validate (read-back)Paraphrase the problem, goals, and constraints; correct misunderstandings live.
7Capture outputFill the discovery summary; assign open questions and next steps with owners.

❓Sample Questions to Ask Stakeholders

Problem & goal
  • "Walk me through how this task is done today, end to end."
  • "What does success look like in six months? How would we measure it?"
  • "What happens today when it goes wrong?" (5 Whys)
Constraints & data
  • "What data do we already have, and are we permitted to use it?"
  • "Are there regulatory or privacy rules that apply here?"
  • "What's fixed — the budget, the deadline, or the scope?"
  • "Where must a human stay in the loop?"

🏢Real-World Example / Mini Case Study

Case Study

From fuzzy ask to scoped project. Running the seven steps on "help our support team," the architect uses the 5 Whys to discover the pain is search time, not reply quality. Success is quantified (−25% handling time). A constraint probe reveals GDPR applies and the KB export needs Compliance sign-off (logged as an open question, not a blocker). The read-back corrects a leader's assumption that the tool would "answer customers directly" — it will assist agents, keeping a human in the loop. The completed discovery summary becomes the first input to the SRS the next day.

💡Tips & Tricks

💡 Session-running tips
  • Keep a visible parking lot and open-questions list on screen — it stops derailment and captures follow-ups.
  • Timebox ruthlessly; if the problem isn't clear, don't let the group jump to design.
  • End every session with a spoken read-back and named owners for each next step.
  • Turn the discovery summary into the requirements doc within 24 hours, while context is fresh.

⚠️Common Pitfalls

  • No prepared agenda or objective — the session drifts.
  • Letting solutioning hijack problem exploration.
  • Not writing the summary — verbal agreement evaporates by the next meeting.
  • Leaving open questions without owners or dates.
  • Treating an open question as a hard blocker and stalling the whole project.

🎯Exam Strategy

Hands-on items are usually ordering ("what is the correct sequence of steps?") or best-next-action in a session gone wrong. Anchor on the canonical order: prepare → frame → explore problem → define success → surface constraints → validate → document. If the scenario shows the team designing before the problem or success is defined, the right answer pulls them back to understand and validate first. Look for the artifact keyword "discovery summary" as the expected output.

Chapter Summary & Key Takeaways

  • Understand before you build. Discovery exists to find the real problem; premature solutioning is the cardinal sin.
  • Map every stakeholder by power & interest — talk to end users and SMEs, not just the sponsor.
  • Active listening & read-back is the cheapest, most powerful defect-detector in requirements work.
  • Translate business language into testable requirements — replace every vague adjective with a metric and threshold.
  • Distinguish FRs (what it does) from NFRs (how well/under what constraint), and keep traceability to business drivers.
  • Surface constraints early — for AI, data availability and compliance are usually the binding ones, not compute.
  • Write SMART, versioned requirements documents that include AI-specific data and evaluation sections.
  • The discovery summary is the bridge artifact from a fuzzy ask to a scoped, documented AI project.

Self-check Quiz

Attempt each question before expanding the answer.

During a kickoff, a stakeholder says "just build us an LLM chatbot." What should the architect do first?
Explore and validate the underlying problem first. Park the proposed solution and use open questions / 5 Whys to understand the real need and success criteria before committing to any technology. Premature solutioning is the key pitfall.
Classify: "The assistant must return a response with P95 latency under 2 seconds." FR or NFR?
Non-functional requirement (NFR). It describes a quality attribute (performance) — how well the system behaves — not a distinct behaviour. Contrast with an FR such as "the assistant shall classify each ticket into a category."
Rewrite "the model should be accurate" as a SMART requirement.
e.g. "The classifier shall achieve ≥ 92% top-1 accuracy on the 5,000-record held-out test set, verified in staging before the 30 June release." It is Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound — and names the evaluation set.
A scenario mentions "only 200 labelled records" for a category. Which constraint dominates, and how should the approach adapt?
Data availability is the binding constraint. Prefer few-shot prompting or retrieval-augmented generation over fine-tuning, and add a data-collection/labelling plan — more compute or budget won't fix insufficient data.
Why include an explicit "out-of-scope" section in a requirements document?
To prevent scope creep. Stating what will not be built is as important as stating what will — it sets clear boundaries and a shared baseline for change control.
What is a requirements traceability matrix (RTM) used for?
It links each requirement → business driver → design element → test case, so every requirement is justified and verifiable, and the impact of any change can be assessed quickly.
Put the discovery-session steps in the correct order.
Prepare → Open & frame → Explore the problem → Define success → Surface constraints → Validate (read-back) → Capture output (discovery summary). Problem understanding and success definition always precede constraints and documentation.
In a power/interest grid, how do you engage a high-power, high-interest stakeholder?
Manage closely — engage frequently and collaboratively (e.g. the accountable sponsor and key SMEs). High-power/low-interest → keep satisfied; low-power/high-interest → keep informed; low-power/low-interest → monitor.
Compliance requirements are discovered only after the model is built. Why is this a serious pitfall?
It causes the most expensive rework — architecture, data handling, and logging may all need to change to satisfy GDPR/HIPAA/EU-AI-Act obligations. Compliance must be surfaced during discovery, not after build.
What single technique best confirms you understood a stakeholder correctly?
Active-listening read-back: paraphrase the goal/requirement in your own words and ask the stakeholder to confirm or correct it — catching misunderstanding on the spot.

⏱️Time management

  • Budget ~60–90 seconds per scenario MCQ; definitions and classifications should take < 30 s.
  • Read the last sentence (the actual question) first, then scan the scenario for the keyword that answers it.
  • Flag and skip a long scenario you're unsure of; return after banking the quick wins.
  • Eliminate the two obviously-wrong options first — most items reduce to a choice between two.

Engage only the sponsorSponsor has authorityPrefer answers that engage end users & SMEs too — tacit knowledge lives there.
Treat an open question as a blockerFeels cautiousLog it with an owner/date and proceed; don't stall the whole project.
Confuse FR with NFRBoth are "requirements"Ask: is it a behaviour (FR) or a quality/limit (NFR)?
