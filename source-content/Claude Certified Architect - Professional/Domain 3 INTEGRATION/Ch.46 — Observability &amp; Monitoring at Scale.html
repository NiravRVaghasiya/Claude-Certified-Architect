46.1 Observability Challenges in LLM Systems

Traditional observability assumes deterministic services with clear pass/fail outcomes. LLM systems break that assumption — the same input can yield different, and differently correct, outputs.

Core Concepts

  • Monitoring vs. observability. Monitoring tracks known failure modes you predefined (is latency high? is the error rate up?). Observability is the property of being able to ask new questions of your system from its outputs — critical for LLMs, where failures are often novel and semantic rather than a stack trace.
  • The three pillars. Metrics (numeric time series — latency, throughput, error rate, token counts), logs (discrete timestamped events), and traces (the end-to-end path of one request across services). LLM apps add a fourth practical concern: capturing prompts, completions, and quality signals.
  • Non-determinism. With sampling (temperature > 0) the same prompt produces varying outputs, so you cannot assert exact-match correctness. You monitor distributions and quality signals instead of single values.
  • Correctness is fuzzy. A "wrong" answer rarely throws an exception — it returns HTTP 200 with plausible but incorrect content. Standard error-rate dashboards are blind to this silent failure class.
  • Cost and latency are first-class signals. Token consumption drives spend, and latency is highly variable (dependent on output length and load). Both must be observable per request, not just in aggregate.
  • Multi-step pipelines. Agents, tool calls, and retrieval (RAG) chain many operations. Without distributed tracing you cannot tell which step in a chain caused a bad answer.

💡 Tip: Anchor the whole chapter on one distinction — infrastructure signals (latency, errors, throughput, cost) behave like any web service and are easy to monitor, while semantic signals (answer quality, hallucination, relevance, safety) are LLM-specific and need evaluation, not just counters. Nearly every exam question maps back to "which category is this?"
⚠️ Common Pitfall: Assuming a healthy error-rate and latency dashboard means the system is "working." An LLM can be fast, cheap, and returning 200s while producing hallucinated or off-topic answers. Green infra dashboards do not imply good outputs. Treating uptime as a proxy for quality is a classic distractor.
🎯 Exam Strategy:

This subtopic is usually tested with definition-matching and "why is LLM observability different?" conceptual questions. Expect them to contrast deterministic and probabilistic systems, or to ask which pillar (metric/log/trace) answers a given question.

Reasoning: All three cited signals are infrastructure signals — they say nothing about output quality. The gap is the absence of a semantic/quality signal (relevance or answer-quality evaluation). The exam wants you to recognize that infra health ≠ output correctness, and pick the "no output-quality monitoring" answer over tempting distractors like "scale up compute" or "increase timeout."

46.2 Selecting Monitoring Strategies for Production

Choosing what to measure and how to structure alerts. Established SRE frameworks give you a scaffold; LLM-specific quality metrics fill the gap they leave.

Core Concepts

  • Golden signals (Google SRE). Four service-health signals: latency, traffic, errors, and saturation. A solid, vendor-neutral starting point for any request-driven service, LLM apps included.
  • RED method. Rate (requests/sec), Errors, Duration — a request-centric view well suited to API-style LLM endpoints.
  • USE method. Utilization, Saturation, Errors — a resource-centric view for the infrastructure a service runs on.
  • LLM-specific metrics layered on top. Token usage (input/output), cost per request, time-to-first-token (TTFT) for streaming, and quality/eval scores. These have no analogue in classic web monitoring and must be added deliberately.
  • SLIs, SLOs, and error budgets. An SLI is a measured indicator (e.g., p95 latency, % answers passing an eval); an SLO is the target for it; the error budget is the allowed shortfall. Define these before wiring alerts so thresholds are principled, not arbitrary.
  • Percentiles over averages. Latency is skewed — report p50/p95/p99, not the mean, which hides tail pain that real users feel.
  • Sampling strategy. Full-fidelity capture of every prompt/completion is expensive at scale. Decide up front on sampling (e.g., log all errors, sample a percentage of successes) balanced against cost and debuggability.

💡 Tip: Remember the pairing — RED = request-centric, USE = resource-centric. If the stem is about API traffic and response times, think RED. If it's about a saturated GPU, queue, or connection pool, think USE. Golden Signals is the "everything, lightly" default.
⚠️ Common Pitfall: Alerting on averages. A mean latency that looks fine can mask a slow tail where p99 users are timing out. Equally, setting SLOs after building dashboards leads to arbitrary thresholds — define the SLI/SLO first, then instrument to it.
🎯 Exam Strategy:

Expect best-practice selection and framework-matching questions: given a scenario, pick the right method or the right metric. They also test SLI/SLO/error-budget vocabulary directly.

Practice question You are asked to define alerting for a streaming Claude endpoint where users care most about responsiveness. Which single indicator best captures perceived responsiveness for streaming, and at which statistic?

Reasoning: For streaming, time-to-first-token reflects when the user starts seeing output, and you should track a tail percentile (p95/p99) rather than the average — a good mean with a bad tail still frustrates users. The exam contrasts TTFT (streaming-relevant) with total generation latency, and rewards choosing percentiles over means.

46.3 Logging Patterns: What to Capture and What to Omit

Logging LLM traffic is a balance between debuggability and risk. Prompts and completions are the richest debugging asset and the highest-risk data you handle.

Core Concepts

  • Structured logging. Emit machine-parseable records (e.g., JSON) with consistent fields, not free-text lines. Structured logs are queryable, aggregatable, and traceable.
  • Correlation IDs. Attach a request/trace ID to every log line so you can reconstruct a single request's journey across services and tie logs to traces.
  • Capture the operational envelope. Metadata that is safe and high-value: timestamps, model version/ID, prompt-template ID, token counts, latency, finish reason, retry counts, error codes, and eval/quality scores.
  • Handle content carefully. Raw prompts and completions can contain PII, secrets, or proprietary data. Redact, sanitize, or sample rather than logging everything verbatim.
  • Redaction / scrubbing. Log redaction and log scrubbing are used synonymously here — both mean removing or masking sensitive fields (PII, credentials) before persistence. Prefer redacting at capture time, not after storage.
  • Retention and access control. Set retention windows appropriate to data sensitivity and compliance obligations, and restrict who can read raw content logs.
  • Log levels & volume. Use levels (DEBUG/INFO/WARN/ERROR) deliberately; verbose per-request content logging at full fidelity is expensive at scale — sample successes, keep full detail on errors.

💡 Tip: Separate what happened (metadata: always safe, always log) from what was said (content: prompts/completions — sensitive, redact and sample). If a question asks what to log "by default at scale," the answer leans toward structured metadata plus redacted/sampled content, never raw verbatim content for every request.
⚠️ Common Pitfall: Logging full prompts and completions verbatim "so we can debug later." This creates a PII/secret liability, inflates storage cost, and can violate data-handling policies. Also beware: redacting after writing to storage still leaves the raw data exposed in the interim — redact at capture time.
🎯 Exam Strategy:

Tested via scenario-based "what should / shouldn't be logged" questions and best-practice selection. Watch for compliance-flavored stems and terminology (redaction = scrubbing).

Signal phrases to watch for:

Practice question A team wants to log every prompt and completion in full to aid debugging of a customer-facing Claude app. What is the primary risk, and what is the better pattern?

Reasoning: The primary risk is capturing PII/secrets in content logs (a privacy/compliance and cost liability). The better pattern is structured metadata for every request plus redacted and sampled content, with correlation IDs, access controls, and defined retention. The exam rewards "redact + sample + control access," not "log everything" or "log nothing."

46.4 Alerting on Model Quality Degradation

The hardest LLM monitoring problem: catching output quality getting worse when nothing "errors." This is where evaluation-based signals and drift detection earn their place.

Core Concepts

  • Infrastructure drift vs. semantic drift. Infrastructure drift = latency, error, cost changes. Semantic drift = output quality changes (relevance, correctness, hallucination rate, tone/safety). They need different detectors and different metrics.
  • Sources of degradation. Input distribution shift (users ask new kinds of questions), prompt/template changes, model or version changes, upstream data/RAG source changes, and configuration drift. Instrument so you can attribute a drop to one of these.
  • Quality signals to alert on. Automated eval scores on sampled traffic, guardrail/violation rates, refusal or "I don't know" rates, user feedback (thumbs up/down), fallback and retry rates, and output-format validation failures.
  • Automated evaluation. Score sampled production outputs against reference criteria — using rule-based checks, classifiers, or an LLM-as-judge — to produce a trackable quality metric. Treat judge scores as noisy proxies, not ground truth.
  • Detection vs. remediation. Alerting tells you quality dropped (detection). Remediation is the response — rollback, revert a prompt, switch models, or route to a fallback. Exams frequently pit these two against each other.
  • Baselines & thresholds. Alert on deviation from an established baseline (e.g., a sustained drop in pass-rate over a window), not on a single noisy sample. Use windows and sustained-breach conditions to avoid alert fatigue.
  • Canary & guardrails. Roll changes out to a small slice first and compare quality against the baseline before full deployment; layer input/output guardrails to catch unsafe or malformed responses in real time.

💡 Tip: When monitoring LLM quality degradation, distinguish between infrastructure drift (latency, errors) and semantic drift (output quality, hallucination rate) — exams often test whether you pick the correct metric category. A follow-on trap tests whether you know the alert is only detection; the fix (rollback/retrain/reroute) is remediation.

🎯 Exam Strategy:

Look for whether the question is asking about detection (alerting) vs. remediation (rollback/retraining) — these are commonly confused distractors. Also common: classify a symptom as infrastructure vs. semantic drift.

Practice question Automated evals show answer-quality pass-rate dropped from a stable baseline after a prompt-template update, while latency and errors are unchanged. What does the alert indicate, and what is the appropriate first action?

Reasoning: Unchanged latency/errors but falling eval pass-rate points to semantic drift, likely caused by the template change. The alert is detection only; the appropriate first remediation is to roll back / revert the prompt change and re-measure. The exam rewards separating "what the alert tells you" (detection of semantic drift) from "what you do about it" (remediation), and correctly attributing cause to the recent change.

46.5 Hands-On: Building an Observability Stack

Assembling the pieces into a coherent pipeline: instrument → collect → store → visualize → alert. The tools below are illustrative categories; the exam tests the architecture and roles, not any single vendor.

Core Concepts

  • Instrumentation layer. Emit metrics, logs, and traces from the application. Open, vendor-neutral instrumentation standards (for example, OpenTelemetry) let you decouple what you emit from where it's stored.
  • Collection / pipeline. A collector or agent receives telemetry, processes it (batching, sampling, redaction), and forwards it to backends. This is the natural place to enforce redaction before storage.
  • Storage backends. Different signal types suit different stores: time-series databases for metrics, log/search stores for logs, and trace stores for traces. LLM-specific eval results are typically stored alongside as metrics or structured records.
  • Visualization & dashboards. Dashboards turn stored signals into human-readable views — separate infra dashboards (latency, errors, cost, tokens) from quality dashboards (eval pass-rate, feedback, guardrail hits).
  • Alerting layer. Rules evaluate metrics/eval scores against SLOs and route notifications. Configure sustained-breach windows to reduce noise.
  • Evaluation pipeline. An offline/online loop that samples production traffic, scores it, and feeds results back as a monitorable quality metric — the LLM-specific addition to the classic stack.
  • Trace-driven debugging. With distributed traces and correlation IDs, you can drill from an alert to the exact request, view its full chain (retrieval, tool calls, model call), and find the failing step.

💡 Tip: Memorize the five-stage backbone — Instrument → Collect → Store → Visualize → Alert — and remember the LLM twist: an evaluation loop feeds a semantic-quality metric into the same Store/Visualize/Alert stages. If a question drops a tool into the wrong stage (e.g., "use the dashboard tool to redact PII"), that mismatch is usually the wrong answer.
⚠️ Common Pitfall: Coupling your app tightly to one vendor's SDK, making backends hard to swap; or performing redaction only in the visualization layer (too late — raw data was already stored). Redaction belongs in the collection/instrumentation stage. Another trap: building only infra dashboards and omitting the evaluation loop, so semantic drift stays invisible.
🎯 Exam Strategy:

Tested with architecture / component-role questions ("which layer does X?") and best-practice ordering. They favor vendor-neutral, open-standard designs and correct placement of concerns (e.g., where redaction happens).

Signal phrases to watch for:

Practice question Your architecture must guarantee that PII never lands in persistent log storage. At which stage of the observability pipeline should redaction occur, and why?

Reasoning: Redaction must occur at instrumentation/collection time — before the data reaches storage — so raw PII is never persisted. Doing it in the visualization or query layer is too late (the raw data already exists at rest). The exam rewards "redact upstream, at capture/collection," and penalizes "redact in the dashboard."
