29.1 Foundations of Information Provenance

⚡ Quick Reference
  • Provenance = the traceable record of where a piece of information came from and how it was transformed.
  • Three layers: origin (source), derivation (transformations), attribution (the claim↔source link surfaced to the user).
  • Goal: any output claim can be traced back to a verifiable input.

Concept Explanation

Information provenance is the documented lineage of a fact: the source it originated from, every transformation it passed through (extraction, summarisation, paraphrase, aggregation), and the attribution eventually shown to the reader. In an LLM synthesis workflow, provenance is the discipline of never losing the thread between an output sentence and the input passage that justifies it.

Why It Matters

Users can only trust a synthesised answer if they can verify it. Regulated domains (medical, legal, financial) require attributable claims; enterprise deployments demand auditability. Provenance is also the primary defence against hallucination — a claim with no traceable source is a red flag by construction. On the exam, provenance is the conceptual foundation the remaining six subsections build on.

Detailed Breakdown

  • Origin metadata — document title, author, publisher, URL, publication date, retrieval date.
  • Derivation chain — the ordered transformations: retrieve → chunk → extract → paraphrase → aggregate. Each step can degrade fidelity.
  • Attribution surface — how provenance is exposed: inline citations, footnotes, hover excerpts, or a source appendix.
  • Granularity — provenance can be tracked per-document, per-passage, per-sentence, or per-claim. Finer granularity = stronger verifiability but higher overhead.
  • Provenance loss — the failure mode where paraphrasing or aggregation strips the link between a claim and its source.

💡 Tips & Tricks
  • Capture provenance at retrieval time and carry a stable source ID through every downstream step — never reconstruct it after paraphrasing.
  • Track provenance at the claim level, not the document level, so each sentence stays independently verifiable.
  • Memorise the derivation chain verbs (retrieve, extract, paraphrase, aggregate) — exam distractors often reorder them.
⚠️ Common Pitfalls
  • Treating provenance as a citation formatting task rather than an end-to-end lineage discipline.
  • Assuming a document-level citation is “good enough” when a claim needs passage-level support.
  • Letting aggregation merge two sources into one sentence without preserving both source IDs.
🎯 Exam Strategy:

Expect definition-matching and “which layer is this?” items. If a question describes a fact that can no longer be traced to its source after summarisation, the correct term is provenance loss, not “hallucination” — distinguish a broken link (provenance) from an invented fact (hallucination).

29.2 Claim–Source Mapping & Attribution

⚡ Quick Reference
  • Claim–source mapping = a table linking each extracted claim to the exact source passage that supports it.
  • A valid mapping has four columns: claim, source, locator (URL/section), supporting excerpt.
  • Rule: one claim → at least one verbatim excerpt. No excerpt = unsupported.

Concept Explanation

Attribution turns provenance from an internal record into a user-facing guarantee. Claim–source mapping is the concrete artefact: for every atomic claim in the output, you record which source and which specific excerpt justify it. It converts “the model said so” into “this passage says so.”

Why It Matters

Attribution is what auditors and end users actually inspect. A well-formed mapping lets a reviewer spot-check any sentence in seconds and is the difference between a demo and a deployable system. It is also the antidote to attribution drift — where paraphrase gradually detaches a claim from its evidence.

Detailed Breakdown

  • Atomic claims — decompose compound sentences so each testable fact maps independently.
  • Locators — a citation must point to something checkable: URL + anchor, page, or paragraph, not just a domain.
  • Verbatim excerpts — quote the supporting text so the reader verifies the model’s reading, not just the link.
  • Support strength — direct (states the claim), indirect (implies it), none (fabricated / over-reached).
  • Multi-source claims — a single claim may need several excerpts; list all of them.

💡 Tips & Tricks
  • Split every “X and Y” sentence into two rows — compound claims hide unsupported halves.
  • If you can’t paste a verbatim excerpt, the claim is unsupported. Excerpt-or-drop is the fastest self-check.
  • Store the source ID with the claim during extraction so the mapping is a lookup, not a reconstruction.
⚠️ Common Pitfalls
  • Citing the domain instead of the passage (“see example.org” proves nothing).
  • Mapping a paraphrase to a source that only implies it, then labelling it as direct support.
  • Dropping attribution during the final polish pass because it “reads better” without citations.
🎯 Exam Strategy:

Questions often present a summarised paragraph and ask which claim lost its source attribution. Look for absolute statements without hedging and forward-looking projections — these are the claims most likely to have no excerpt behind them. The answer is usually the sentence you cannot map to a quotable line.

29.3 Conflict Detection & Resolution

⚡ Quick Reference
  • Sources disagree constantly — detect the conflict, then resolve transparently rather than silently picking one.
  • Resolution ladder: reconcile → prefer → present-both → flag-uncertain.
  • Golden rule: never average away a genuine disagreement; disclose it.

Concept Explanation

When two credible sources report different numbers for the same fact, the synthesis system must detect the discrepancy and decide how to handle it. Conflict resolution is choosing a principled response — reconcile the difference, prefer the stronger source, present both, or flag the item as contested — instead of quietly emitting one figure as if it were undisputed.

Why It Matters

Silent conflict resolution is a top trust failure: the user never learns the number was disputed. Handling conflict well is what separates a synthesiser from a source-picker. This subsection aligns with 29.6: an unresolved conflict must be reflected as lowered confidence and hedged language in the final output, never as a false-precision single value.

Detailed Breakdown

  • Detect — normalise units, dates, and definitions before comparing; many “conflicts” are unit or scope mismatches.
  • Diagnose — is the difference from methodology, time period, population, or definition?
  • Resolution ladder:
    • Reconcile — the values agree once normalised (e.g., fiscal vs calendar year).
    • Prefer — one source is clearly more authoritative/recent (see 29.4).
    • Present both — both credible and genuinely differ; show the range.
    • Flag uncertain — cannot adjudicate; label as contested and lower confidence.
SourceStat ClaimedDateMethodology Note (example only)
WHO (illustrative)62% adoption rate2023Survey-based, self-reported
World Bank (illustrative)71% adoption rate2023Administrative / registry data
Figure 29.3 — Same metric, same year, ~9 pt gap driven by methodology. Correct output: “estimates range 62–71% depending on methodology,” not a single averaged figure.
💡 Tips & Tricks
  • Normalise before you compare — unit/scope mismatches masquerade as conflicts.
  • When two credible sources genuinely differ, present a range with attribution rather than choosing.
  • Tie the resolution to a confidence signal (29.6): “present-both” and “flag-uncertain” should both lower stated confidence.
⚠️ Common Pitfalls
  • Silent selection — emitting one figure and hiding that a conflict existed.
  • False averaging — reporting 66.5% for a 62 vs 71 dispute, inventing a number no source states.
  • Comparing figures with different denominators, dates, or definitions and calling the mismatch a conflict.
🎯 Exam Strategy:

You’ll be shown two conflicting stats and asked for the best response. Eliminate “pick the higher/lower value” and “average them” — both are traps. The correct answer almost always preserves the disagreement (present both with attribution) or investigates the methodology gap first.

29.4 Source Credibility & Reliability Assessment

⚡ Quick Reference
  • Not all sources are equal — weight by authority, recency, primacy, independence, and corroboration.
  • Primary (original data) > secondary (analysis) > tertiary (aggregation).
  • Credibility feeds the “prefer” rung of the 29.3 conflict ladder.

Concept Explanation

Credibility assessment ranks sources so the system knows which to trust when they disagree. It combines the source’s authority and independence with contextual factors like recency and whether other independent sources corroborate the claim. It is judgment made explicit and repeatable.

Why It Matters

Credibility is the input that makes “prefer the stronger source” (29.3) principled rather than arbitrary. Weighting sources poorly propagates errors into every downstream claim, and over-trusting a single high-authority source can be as damaging as treating a blog post like a peer-reviewed study.

Detailed Breakdown

  • Authority — expertise, editorial standards, track record of the publisher.
  • Primacy — primary > secondary > tertiary; closer to the original data is stronger.
  • Recency — matters most for fast-moving facts; least for stable/historical facts.
  • Independence — two outlets both citing one wire story are not independent corroboration.
  • Corroboration — independent agreement raises confidence; echo-chamber repetition does not.
  • Conflict of interest — flag self-serving sources (vendor claims about their own product).

💡 Tips & Tricks
  • Ask “how many independent origins?” — three reposts of one story count as one source.
  • Match recency weight to the fact’s volatility; don’t penalise a stable historical figure for being old.
  • Record a short credibility rationale per source so “prefer” decisions in 29.3 are auditable.
⚠️ Common Pitfalls
  • Authority bias — assuming a big-name source is right even when a primary dataset disagrees.
  • Counting correlated sources as independent corroboration (circular sourcing).
  • Over-weighting recency for facts that don’t change, or under-weighting it for facts that do.
🎯 Exam Strategy:

Watch for the independence trap: a scenario gives “five sources agree,” but they all trace to one press release. The credible answer treats them as a single source. Also distinguish primary vs secondary — the exam rewards preferring the original data over a well-written summary of it.

29.5 Synthesis & Aggregation Techniques

⚡ Quick Reference
  • Synthesis = combining multiple sources into one coherent, attributed answer — not concatenation.
  • Patterns: consensus, contrast, chronological, thematic, weighted.
  • Every synthesised sentence must retain its source link (ties back to 29.1/29.2).

Concept Explanation

Synthesis merges facts from many sources into a unified narrative while preserving attribution and disagreement. Good synthesis reads as one voice yet lets the reader trace each claim; poor synthesis either stitches sources together verbatim (concatenation) or blends them until provenance is lost.

Why It Matters

Synthesis is where provenance is most likely to break: aggregating across sources tempts the model to merge, average, and paraphrase away the links established in 29.1–29.2. It is also where conflict handling (29.3) and confidence (29.6) become visible in the actual prose the user reads.

Detailed Breakdown

  • Consensus synthesis — state what sources agree on, cite the strongest few.
  • Contrast synthesis — foreground disagreement; ideal when 29.3 returns “present both.”
  • Chronological — order by time for evolving stories (each stage attributed).
  • Thematic — group by sub-topic rather than by source document.
  • Weighted — lean on higher-credibility sources (29.4) while still noting minority views.
  • Attribution retention — carry source IDs through the merge; re-attach citations after paraphrase.
TechniqueBest when…Attribution risk
ConsensusSources largely agreeMedium — may hide the dissenting source
ContrastGenuine disagreement (29.3)Low — keeps both sources visible
ChronologicalFacts evolve over timeMedium — mixing dates loses per-stage source
WeightedUneven source qualityHigh — minority view can vanish
Figure 29.5 — Synthesis patterns and their attribution-loss risk. Match the pattern to the source relationship.
💡 Tips & Tricks
  • Choose the pattern from the source relationship: agreement → consensus, disagreement → contrast.
  • Paraphrase last, cite immediately after — re-attach the source ID the moment you reword a claim.
  • Synthesis ≠ summary: a summary compresses one source; synthesis reconciles many.
⚠️ Common Pitfalls
  • Concatenation disguised as synthesis — pasting source blurbs back to back.
  • Blending sources so smoothly that individual claims can no longer be attributed.
  • Using weighted synthesis to silently erase a credible minority view.
🎯 Exam Strategy:

Given a source relationship, pick the matching synthesis pattern. If sources conflict, the right technique is contrast (consistent with 29.3’s “present both”), not consensus. Distinguish synthesis (many sources reconciled) from summarisation (one source compressed) — the exam tests this pair directly.

29.6 Uncertainty, Hedging & Confidence Calibration

⚡ Quick Reference
  • Calibration = language confidence should match evidence strength — no false precision, no false doubt.
  • Hedging is a signal, not filler: “estimates suggest,” “reported by X,” “contested.”
  • Unresolved conflict (29.3)  ⇒  lower confidence + hedged wording. These must agree.

Concept Explanation

Confidence calibration is matching the certainty of the wording to the strength of the underlying evidence. A well-supported, corroborated claim can be stated plainly; a single-source or contested claim should carry hedging that signals the limitation to the reader.

Why It Matters

Miscalibration erodes trust in both directions: overconfidence presents shaky claims as fact, while excessive hedging buries solid conclusions in qualifiers. This subsection is the linguistic output of 29.3 — the resolution ladder’s “present both” and “flag uncertain” must surface here as visibly reduced confidence. If 29.3 says contested and 29.6 says “definitely,” the system contradicts itself.

Detailed Breakdown

  • Confidence tiers — well-established → likely → contested → speculative.
  • Hedging vocabulary — map each tier to phrasing (“is” / “appears to” / “is reported to” / “may”).
  • Attribution as hedge — “According to Source X…” both cites and scopes the claim.
  • Confidence ≠ probability — hedging communicates evidential support, not a precise numeric likelihood.
  • Consistency contract — a claim marked contested in the mapping (29.2) must read as contested in prose.
Confidence TierEvidence PatternRecommended Phrasing
Well-establishedMultiple independent, corroborating sources“X is …” (state plainly)
LikelyStrong single or partly corroborated source“X appears to … / likely …”
ContestedCredible sources disagree (from 29.3)“Estimates range … depending on source”
SpeculativeWeak / single / self-interested source“One source suggests … (unverified)”
Figure 29.6 — Evidence tiers mapped to calibrated language. The “Contested” row is the direct output of a 29.3 “present both” decision.
💡 Tips & Tricks
  • Set the confidence tier before writing the sentence, then pick phrasing from the table — don’t hedge by instinct.
  • Use attribution (“per Source X”) as your default hedge; it cites and calibrates in one move.
  • Cross-check 29.3 and 29.6: any “present both” / “flag uncertain” outcome must read as contested here.
⚠️ Common Pitfalls
  • False precision — stating a contested figure as a single confident number.
  • Hedge everything — qualifying well-established facts until the answer is useless.
  • Marking a claim contested in the mapping but writing it confidently in prose (29.3/29.6 contradiction).
🎯 Exam Strategy:

Items give a claim + its evidence and ask you to pick the best-calibrated wording. Match evidence strength to tier: single/contested evidence stated as fact is wrong; a corroborated fact drowned in hedges is also wrong. The right answer’s confidence level mirrors the conflict outcome from 29.3.

29.7 Hands-On: Building a Provenance-Aware Synthesis Pipeline

⚡ Quick Reference
  • End-to-end flow: Sources → Claim extraction → Source mapping → Conflict resolution → Calibrated structured output.
  • Each stage preserves the source ID established at ingestion (29.1).
  • The output is a structured object where every claim carries its sources and a confidence tier.

Concept Explanation

This subsection integrates 29.1–29.6 into one runnable pipeline. Each earlier concept becomes a pipeline stage, and provenance is the invariant that must survive from ingestion to final output.

Why It Matters

The exam’s hands-on items test whether you can sequence the stages correctly and identify where provenance breaks. Getting the order right — and knowing that conflict resolution precedes final wording — is the core competency this chapter certifies.

Pipeline Overview

💡 Tips & Tricks
  • Build the working table (claim · source · conflict · resolution · confidence) before writing prose — the paragraph falls out of it.
  • Keep the source ID column intact from Step 1 to the JSON — that column is your provenance.
  • Verify the invariant: every claim in the final JSON has ≥1 source and a confidence tier, or it doesn’t ship.
⚠️ Common Pitfalls
  • Writing the answer before resolving conflicts, so the prose states 62% (or an averaged 66.5%) as fact.
  • Letting the speculative projection (C3) inherit the confident tone of C1.
  • Dropping the source array during the “make it read nicely” pass.
🎯 Exam Strategy:

Hands-on items scramble the stage order or omit one. The correct sequence is fixed: ingest → extract → map → resolve → synthesise/calibrate. If asked “where did provenance break?”, trace the source ID column — the break is the first stage where a claim lost its source array.
